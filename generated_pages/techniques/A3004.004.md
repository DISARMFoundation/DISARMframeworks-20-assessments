# Assessment A3004.004: Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act

**Summary**: <b>Summary:</b><br><i>Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</i> describes an actor identifying a person to an audience in circumstances where that audience is likely to act against them as a consequence of that identification. For example, posting a target to a forum whose members are known to conduct raids, an account with a large following inciting outrage at a target, publishing their name and employer, and a head of state characterising a journalist as a criminal. What identifies the Technique is that the actor supplies the target without needing an explicit call to harass; the audience of their identification goes on to supply the harassment, leaving the actor without direct culpability for what follows.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br><i>Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</i> is one form of harassment, and is something that often leads to further harassment from said audience. An incident has to satisfy four conditions before it is recorded as this Technique.<br><br><b>A target.</b> There is a target, and it is a person, a group of people, or an institution. The target is identified by the actor in some way to an audience. The target doesn't require being explicitly named as long as they are identifiable to the audience.<br><br><b>Identification to an audience.</b> The actor communicated the target's identity, or information sufficient to locate or reach them, to people other than the target. An account handle, a photograph, an employer, a school, a home address, and a schedule are each sufficient identifiers on their own, and in most cases the actor supplies several.<br><br><b>The audience is not one the actor explicitly coordinates for harassment campaigns.</b> The audience must be one the actor has access to rather than one the actor explicitly runs. An actor who circulates a target list inside a network they trained and can task has not deployed this Technique; they have coordinated the deployment of <b>A3004.002: Harass en Masse</b>. Part of the essence of <b>A3004.004: Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</b> is that an actor isn't explicitly coordinating harassment. There is plausible deniability, but the identification can be assessed to have led to harassment of the target.<br><br>By identifying a target to an audience likely to act on that target (without having an operational relationship with the audience) an actor maintains plausible deniability, as an audience ostensibly acts on its own motivation. This identification can be a form of harassment, which instigates mass harassment to the identified target by the audience of the identification. The deniability of culpability is a unique outcome of this Technique, which is not possible for traditional coordination in which an actor explicitly organises a collaborating audience to participate in mass harassment of a target.<br><br><b>Awareness of the consequence of identification to the audience, subjective or objective.</b> Either the actor intentionally sought an audience to act against the target, or a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew would have understood the audience to be likely to act. The first criterion covers cases where an analyst is able to identify an actor's explicit motive (e.g. through actor statements). The second covers actors who published a name to a community who went on to harass, while the actor claims no responsibility or awareness, where a reasonable person could have predicted to harass the target based on the identification.<br><br>The second branch works from information the actor had available to them rather than from what they say they intended to happen. It asks what a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew would have understood. The first time an actor names a target and a mob arrives, the claim not to have expected it is available to them. By the tenth time it is not, because the pattern is part of the information they hold. For analysts, establishing that a publication caused a specific threat is challenging, but establishing that an actor has named targets repeatedly, and that harassment has repeatedly followed, is more achievable.<br><br>There are actors who, aware of the potential for their posts to lead to mass harassment, take steps to reduce the chance this happens. Callout posts can carry an instruction not to contact the person, a screenshot can have identifying details removed, conduct can be described without the person being named, and a target can be given notice before publication. Fanlore (a wiki site dedicated to fandoms) records that [many callout posts of the 2020s carry the phrase "do not harass this user"](https://fanlore.org/wiki/Callout_Culture) as a direction to block rather than engage. Whether such a disclaimer works is disputed, and an analyst should not treat it as though it does. What it does establish is that the actor contemplated the harassment, since nobody warns against a consequence they have not thought of.<br><br><b>How the Literature Refers to This Behaviour:</b><br>There is no settled term for this Technique in the literature. The nearest named concept is "stochastic terrorism", which describes the same target identification but resulting in physical violence rather than harassment. Some define stochastic terrorism as [the use of mass media to provoke acts that are statistically predictable but individually unpredictable](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17539153.2024.2305742), and others describe it as [raising the probability of violence without direct commands, through a diffusion of responsibility](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ftpvxx/v36y2024i2p234-245.html). This definition has been further developed to include repetition, describing it as the [repeated use of vilifying rhetoric by a public figure that inspires supporters to act](https://www.britannica.com/topic/stochastic-terrorism), and notes that the arrangement generally fails the legal test for incitement because the speech lacks sufficient specificity.<br><br>A criticism of the concept of stochastic terrorism is that it lets an accuser assert causation without demonstrating it, and that the accusation of stochastic terrorism can be used to suppress valid criticism. Analysts making the assessment that this Technique has been deployed will need to be able to justify the link between identification of a target and resulting harassment, and to balance individuals' right to express criticism of named targets with the documented use of target identification to direct harassment.<br><br>Another way this behaviour is referred to is "weaponised visibility" leading to "digital vigilantism", the latter of which is described as [citizens being collectively offended by another citizen's activity and responding through coordinated retaliation](https://philpapers.org/rec/TRODVA). In such a case visibility of the target is itself a weapon used against them.<br><br>Amy Boyer is an individual who has both participated in and been the target of this behaviour which they refer to as "callouts". They describe many callouts as necessary ("people need to be informed of those who are a danger to them"), but also acknowledge that many are abusive, and those that are abusive have common elements. As a consequence of this experience, they have produced [a list of signs that a callout may be abusive](https://medium.com/@amytranscend/theres-been-a-lot-of-important-talk-lately-about-how-callout-culture-can-actually-be-used-to-harm-543b13ce5add).<br><br><b>Examples of Identifying a Target to an Audience Likely to Act:</b><br>In October 2019 Coleen Rooney posted to a combined following of almost two million on Twitter and Instagram that she had planted false stories visible to a single account and watched them reach the press, concluding by naming Rebekah Vardy's account. Vardy sued for libel and lost in July 2022, with [Steyn J finding that the essence of the post was substantially true](https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/12661132/wagatha-christie-rebekah-vardy-loses-libel-case-against-coleen-rooney). Vardy said her family had received abuse and threats as a result, and the same judgment [recognised the vile abuse Vardy had faced and held that nothing she had been accused of could justify subjecting her and her family to such vitriol](https://theconversation.com/wagatha-christie-what-the-judgment-said-and-what-it-means-for-future-libel-litigants-187955). The judge also [agreed that it was not in the public interest for the disclosure to have been made without giving Vardy the chance to respond](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2022-07-30/wagatha-christie-trial-ends-with-rebekah-vardy-backfire/101284902).<br><br>To assess whether this behaviour meets the requirements for this Technique, an analyst must assess whether Rooney knew her behaviour would lead to harassment of Vardy, or that given the information available to her, it's reasonable to say that she should have been aware this would happen. While no court, regulator, or investigator has made a claim to this effect, Rooney published to roughly two million people across three platforms, naming one person as having betrayed her over years, in October 2019, by which point pile-ons following celebrity callouts were well documented. A reasonable person knowing what she knew would plausibly have understood that her audience would be likely to act on her identification. As such, the criteria for applying this Technique is assessed to have been met.<br><br>A second example looks at a case where a government official is repeatedly singled out by an individual leading an organisation the official is attempting to regulate. On 15 April 2024 Bishop Mar Mari Emmanuel was stabbed repeatedly during a livestreamed sermon at the Assyrian Christ the Good Shepherd Church in Wakeley, in western Sydney, and [a riot followed outside the church that night in which police were injured and vehicles damaged](https://www.startupdaily.net/topic/politics-news-analysis/elon-musk-1-esafety-commissioner-0-in-legal-battle-over-priest-stabbing-video-removal-on-x/). A sixteen-year-old was later charged with a federal terrorism offence. Footage circulated immediately on X, and Australia's eSafety Commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, issued removal notices after the material was [classified Class 1 under the Online Safety Act](https://au.news.yahoo.com/musk-court-win-over-stabbing-004100383.html). [Meta, TikTok, Reddit, and Telegram took the video down when asked](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/australia-drops-court-action-x-church-stabbing-posts-rcna155553). X geo-blocked the posts for Australian users and refused to remove them globally, and [eSafety went to court arguing that geo-blocking was not compliant with the law because the content had not been deleted](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-04-22/esafety-commissioner-seeks-injunction-against-x/103755874).<br><br>Throughout the dispute Elon Musk characterised Inman Grant as a global censorship commissar. On the day she discontinued the case against X, she said Musk had ["issued a dog whistle to 181 million users around the globe"](https://region.com.au/esafety-drops-federal-court-action-against-x-with-commissioner-claiming-deaths-threats-made/776431/), producing death threats against her and the doxxing of her family including her three children. She has described threats to her employees, her family, and her own safety, credible enough that she involved federal and local police and changed her movements, and a Columbia University study of technology-facilitated gender-based violence used her as a case study and found her [mentioned in almost 74,000 posts on X ahead of the court proceedings, having been a relatively unknown figure online beforehand](https://ghanaiantimes.com.gh/these-arent-just-words-the-woman-threatened-for-taking-x-to-court/). Both the Communications Minister and the Opposition Leader condemned the abuse in Parliament.<br><br>Musk used the same characterisation of Inman Grant again in a second regulatory dispute eighteen months later. Following the announcement of Australia's under-16 social media ban, which Inman Grant was to implement, he again posted calling her a censorship commissar, and [within 24 hours 75,000 posts were directed at her, 80% of them toxic, harmful, and plausible death threats](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/esafety-commissioner-julie-inman-grant-death-thrats-social-media/t1rkjtpqv). She has since said her own account is dormant and [that eSafety no longer has a handle at all](https://www.forbes.com.au/covers/leadership/inman-grant-the-personal-toll-of-being-elon-musks-censorship-commissar/).<br><br>The assessment of the actor's awareness of the outcome of singling out a target runs differently here. Rooney's case rests on general knowledge about what follows a celebrity callout. Musk's rests on his own record against the same target. The first time he singled out Inman Grant led to death threats, the doxxing of three children, police involvement, and condemnation in Parliament, all of which were public by June 2024. Whatever claim of surprise was available before that was not available in November, because by then the result of characterising this official in this way had been established, reported, and debated in the legislature. Further, as the owner of a social media platform, Musk is more aware than most of the trust and safety issues associated with target identification and their resulting harassment.<br><br>A reasonable person with access to the same information would have understood the audience to be likely to act, and the claim not to have anticipated it is not available to an actor who has watched it happen already. No public evidence establishes that he sought the outcome, but given the information known to be available to him, it does not need to be. As such, the Technique is assessed to have been deployed here. However, that assessment takes no position on the validity of the criticism. Contesting a regulator's exercise of censorship powers is within the bounds of legitimate free speech, and Musk was in litigation with her agency at the time. That and the documented consequence for Inman Grant are both true at once.
