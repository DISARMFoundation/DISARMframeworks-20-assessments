# Assessment A3004.003: Dox

**Summary**: <b>Summary:</b><br><i>Dox</i> describes an actor publishing information about a target which makes them findable, attaches their identity to something they did anonymously, or discloses a private fact whose disclosure carries consequences for them. It covers a home address posted to a forum, a pseudonymous account matched to a legal name, and a person's relationship history circulated in a community where it will cost them. What identifies the Technique is that the actor moved information into a setting the target had kept it out of, rather than saying anything about them.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>Doxxing is one form of harassment, and an incident has to satisfy three conditions before it is recorded as this Technique.<br><br><b>A target.</b> There is a target, and it is a person, a group of people, or an institution. The target is identified by the actor in some way to an audience. The target doesn't require being explicitly named as long as they are identifiable to the audience.<br><br><b>Publication of one of three kinds of information.</b> The actor published or transmitted information about the target to people who did not hold it, and that information is in [at least one of the following forms](https://projects.itforchange.net/online-violence-gender-and-law-guide/module-2-typologies-of-online-gender-based-offenses-in-law/2-8-doxxing/):<br><br><b>Targeting Doxxing:</b> Publishing information which makes the target findable or contactable. Associating with a target a home address, a telephone number, an employer, a school, a vehicle registration, a daily routine, or a photograph of a front door are each examples of ways that a target can be made more findable or contactable.<br><b>Deanonymising Doxxing:</b> Publishing information which attaches the target's identity to something they did anonymously or under a pseudonym, such as a legal name matched to a previously unassociated online account.<br><b>Delegitimising Doxxing:</b> Publishing information which discloses private facts about the target's life whose disclosure carries consequences for a person in their position. Examples include sexual history, relationship or marital status, health, immigration status, religious practice, and the identities of family members. Which of these carries consequences depends on where the target lives, who they are, and factors unique to their culture. An analyst who considers doxxing to only cover information like addresses and telephone numbers can miss this type of doxxing.<br><br><b>Intent to harass, or reasonable understanding of the publication as harassment.</b> Either the actor sought to alarm, distress, frighten, or constrain the target, or a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew, and taking into consideration the position of the target, would have understood the publication to do so. For more information about both the "intent to harass" and "reasonable understanding of behaviour as harassment" paths, see <b>A3004.001: Harass</b>.<br><br>Doxxes do not need to publish information which was previously a secret. Information gathered from open sources and published to an audience who did not previously hold that information can be considered doxxing even though a motivated actor from the same audience could find such information themselves. To identify doxxing [analysts should consider whether something shared somewhere expected, intentional, or desired has been moved somewhere unexpected, unintentional, and undesired.](https://www.nomos-elibrary.de/10.5771/1615-634X-2020-3-273.pdf)<br><br>Doxxing occurred through open source applications known as Sulli Deals and Bulli Bai, hosted on GitHub, which put Indian Muslim women up for mock auction using [photographs sourced from the women's own social media handles without their consent](https://projects.itforchange.net/online-violence-gender-and-law-guide/module-2-typologies-of-online-gender-based-offenses-in-law/2-8-doxxing/). While the information was available on an open source, the women did not intend for the information to be collated and shared to an audience of men on a mock auction website, and its publication there made them identifiable to an audience which they did not intend their information to be provided to.<br><br>The principle is that publishing information in one setting is not consent to its appearing in every setting. Where someone shares information about themselves with a person or group online, they retain the right to control the flow of information pertaining to them. This is already how DISARM defines the Observation <b>T4202.007: Content Constitutes Leak of Private Material</b>; private materials having been non-consensually published to an audience not originally intended, which is a contextual-integrity test rather than a secrecy test. An analyst applying this Technique should read it that way.<br><br>Assessing the context of informational flow helps analysts see that the same information carries different consequences for different people. What may appropriately be revealed about a person depends on the social and political context they live in, so information that is unremarkable for one target may end a career or damage social standing for another. Looking only at whether information was previously private does not identify these contextual factors, which affect the harms and severity of a doxxing.<br><br>While [deanonymising and delegitimising doxxing can serve the public interest by exposing wrongdoing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-016-9406-0), and all three forms of doxxing can be unjustified where they humiliate, threaten, intimidate, or punish the subject.<br><br><b>Doxxing as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>Safeguard Defenders records that an anonymous account [doxxed the old home address of Li Ying, known online as Teacher Li, called on local Chinese students to go and find him, and later published images of his old and new passports](https://safeguarddefenders.com/en/blog/teacher-li-full-transnational-repression-story). He began moving frequently from that point. That case shows some of the harm produced by doxxing. While abuse can be blocked, muted, and ignored, a published address cannot be unpublished, and it does not stop working when the campaign ends; the target's situation changes permanently.<br><br><b>Potential Impacts:</b><br>Impacts are organised by affected party. An item may appear under more than one party where the justification differs.<br><br><b>Primary Target:</b><br><b>A1012.009: Subject Forced to Relocate.</b> Teacher Li began moving frequently once his former address circulated, and relocation is the only remedy available for an address that cannot be recalled.<br><b>A1011.003: Activity Restricts Subject's Freedom of Movement and Residence.</b> Targets change routes, avoid predictable places, and stop attending things once they can be found at them.<br><b>A1011.010: Activity Compromises Subject's Personal Data.</b> Publishing a name, address, or employer discloses personal data without consent, and a published detail cannot be unpublished.<br><b>A1012.006: Subject Killed.</b> Swatting is impossible without an address, and [fatal cases have been documented.](https://rist.tech.cornell.edu/papers/sok-abuse.pdf)<br><b>A1005.006: Activity Exhausts Subject's Resources.</b> Address removal services, legal advice, relocation costs, and physical security are recurring expenses rather than a single response.<br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> The target does not know who holds the information or what any of them will do, and the uncertainty does not end when the campaign does. [IT for Change's guide on online violence, gender, and law](https://projects.itforchange.net/online-violence-gender-and-law-guide/module-2-typologies-of-online-gender-based-offenses-in-law/2-8-doxxing/) lists offline stalking, death and rape threats, harassment of family and employers, physical harm, and the sending of offensive substances to the target's address among the consequences that follow from an address moving.<br><b>A1008.006: Feeling of Anxiety Evoked in Subject.</b> A published doxx remains available and can be acted on years later by people unconnected to the original actor.<br><b>A1010.001: Subject Disengages from Information Ecosystem.</b> [Researchers found that social network accounts named in dox files were more likely to close than typical accounts](https://www.cs.uic.edu/%7Epsnyder/static/papers/fifteen-minutes.pdf).<br><b>A1010.006: Subject Self-Censors when Sharing Information.</b> Targets remove biographical detail, stop posting photographs, and avoid anything that could be matched against what is already out.<br><b>A1011.002: Activity Restricts Subject's Freedom of Expression.</b> The restriction follows from the target's own risk calculation rather than from any prohibition.<br><br><b>People Connected to the Target:</b><br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> Dox files routinely include family members and partners, so people who were never the object of the campaign become reachable through it.<br><b>A1012.009: Subject Forced to Relocate.</b> A household relocates together, so the remedy falls on everyone at the address rather than on the target alone.<br><br><b>Wider Audience:</b><br><b>A1007.002: Activity Dissuades Action.</b> A doxx demonstrates that pseudonymity is not protection, and people who relied on it draw the conclusion.<br><b>A1010.004: Subject Declines to Start Sharing Information.</b> Those who have not yet spoken decide not to, having seen that speaking can be followed by being locatable.<br><b>A1009.002: Degrade Quality of Information Ecosystem.</b> The aggregate of people who withdraw or write anonymously because identification carries physical risk.
