# Assessment A3004.005: Disrupt Event

**Summary**: <b>Summary:</b><br><i>Disrupt Event</i> describes action taken against a specific convened occasion which interferes with that occasion's ability to do what it was convened to do. The action may stop the event happening, prevent it proceeding as planned, or turn it to a purpose other than the one it was called for. In this Technique an event, rather than a person or a body of content, is the thing being acted on.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>For the purposes of this Technique, an event is a bounded occasion which has a convenor, a purpose, an intended audience, and a scheduled time. Conferences, panel discussions, book launches, livestreams, religious services, court hearings, public consultation meetings, graduation ceremonies, ticket sales, and community group meetings all meet this description. An event may be physical, online, or both. Identifying the affected event is a precondition for identifying this Technique. Behaviour which makes life difficult for an organisation in general is not this Technique; behaviour aimed at a particular occasion that organisation had scheduled is.<br><br>An event is disrupted when a participant acts on it in a way that interferes with its ability to fulfil its purpose. The test is functional rather than intentional. An analyst does not need to establish why the actor did it before recording that the Technique was deployed, and tagging this Technique makes no claim as to whether the disruption was just or unjust.<br><br>The Technique covers action at any point in an event's life:<br><br>The event is stopped before it happens. Pressure is applied to the convenor, the venue, the funder, or the host institution, and the occasion is cancelled or the participants who gave it its purpose are removed from it.<br>The event happens but cannot proceed as convened. The proceedings are interrupted, the participants are prevented from hearing or from being heard, or the infrastructure the occasion depends on is attacked.<br>The event happens and serves a purpose other than its own. Control of the occasion passes to the disrupting party, and what the audience receives is what that party brought rather than what the event originally intended to deliver.<br><br>Two situations resemble a disrupted event and are not this Technique. The first is an event that fails with nobody acting on it. A power cut, a speaker's illness, a platform outage, or a venue's own capacity limit will each wreck an occasion without any participant having taken action against it. The second is participation. Attending, listening, asking a hostile question, applauding, refusing to applaud, and walking out are all things audiences do at events, and doing them does not disrupt the occasion. The line falls where a participant's use of the event stops the event working. Asking a long question is participation; holding the floor until the session ends so that nobody else can speak is disruption.<br><br><b>Disrupting an Event as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>In July 2014 the European Association for Chinese Studies held its biennial conference at the University of Minho in Braga, Portugal. On the evening of 22 July, Xu Lin, then Director-General of Confucius Institute Headquarters, reviewed the conference materials and ordered them seized. [Four pages were removed](https://chinesestudies.eu/2014/report-the-deletion-of-pages-from-eacs-conference-materials-in-braga-july-2014/) from the printed programme and abstracts, covering the Chiang Ching-kuo Foundation's presentation of itself as a sponsor, an exhibition by Taiwan's National Central Library, and the associated speaker biographies and event listings. Incomplete materials went out to delegates from 23 July and defaced programmes followed on the morning of 24 July. Roger Greatrex, the EACS president, had 500 replacement pages printed and distributed. This is disruption of an event in progress, carried out on its physical materials. No session was interrupted and nothing was said to the delegates from the platform; what changed was that an attempt was made to make it so that the conference could no longer tell its attendees who had paid for it or what else was on.<br><br>Disruption can also take only part of an event, such as speaker questions. On 24 January 2019 the University of Strasbourg hosted a panel on China's policies in Xinjiang, organised by Thomas Boutonnet, with two Uyghur scholars among the four speakers. The consulate general had already approached the university president to have the event banned, and the president had refused. When the panel opened its audience discussion, [two participants took the available time](https://lingua-sinica.org/the-limits-of-talking-about-china-in-europe/) and left the speakers no opportunity to respond; Boutonnet and colleagues later identified them as visitors from China's consulate general in Strasbourg. Boutonnet let this run for several minutes before intervening. "We need to let others speak," he said. "We have to let the discussion flow. Otherwise, it's simply not possible." No rule of the venue was broken and the event was not stopped, but both the attempts to ban the event and to monopolise the audience questions were done with the goal of disrupting the event by actors associated with Chinese authorities.<br><br>Disruption can target specific parts of an event rather than all of it. In 2024 the Baltic Alliance for Asian Studies conference at the University of Latvia told confirmed participants, less than two weeks before it opened, that they were no longer welcome to present. Raigirdas Boruta, whose paper concerned Taiwan, said the [common element among those rejected](https://lingua-sinica.org/the-limits-of-talking-about-china-in-europe/) was that their work touched subjects sensitive to Beijing. It later emerged that the Chinese Embassy in Riga had funded the conference. The Centre for Asian Studies at Vytautas Magnus University withdrew, and its six scheduled speakers withdrew with it. The conference took place. What did not take place was the portion of it that had been assembled around Taiwan.<br><br>Pressure applied to institutions can lead to events altering their initial intended purpose. ARTICLE 19 has [catalogued European cultural institutions](https://www.article19.org/resources/europe-chinas-censorship-of-cultural-institutions-must-be-challenged/) dropping or changing programming under Chinese pressure, including exhibitions of work by the artist Badiucao in Poland, Italy, and the Czech Republic. The Château des Ducs de Bretagne in Nantes postponed a Genghis Khan exhibition after Chinese partners demanded the removal of words including "Mongolia" and "Empire", and reopened it later without the Chinese partnership.<br><br>Disruption can also be attempted against the technical infrastructure an event runs on. Italian police reported [mitigating denial of service attacks](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/entertainment-arts-61463364) by the pro-Russian group Killnet against network infrastructure during the first semi-final of the 2022 Eurovision Song Contest, in which Ukraine performed, and during the final's voting. The attacks failed. This is the Technique deployed with no impact to record against it; an actor acted on a specific scheduled occasion in a way capable of stopping it working, and the occasion worked anyway.<br><br><b>Disrupting as a Method of Harassment:</b><br>Chen Ling, Utkucan Balcı, Jeremy Blackburn, and Gianluca Stringhini identified [over 200 calls for zoombombing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03822) on Twitter and 4chan across the first seven months of 2020, covering ten online meeting platforms. Their central finding was that most attacks did not come from outsiders guessing meeting IDs. They came from people with legitimate access who published the details and invited attackers in. Password protection and waiting rooms are ineffective against an insider, and the authors found attackers being coached on how to present themselves in order to be admitted.<br><br>On 21 April 2020 the Israeli embassy in Berlin held an online Holocaust Remembrance Day ceremony with the survivor Tswi Joseef Herschel. About twenty minutes in, [anonymous participants posted photographs of Hitler and pornographic images](https://www.timesofisrael.com/anti-semites-break-into-virtual-holocaust-memorial-hosted-by-israeli-embassy/) and shouted antisemitic slogans. The session was suspended and reconvened without them, and a second ceremony was held the following day on Facebook Live behind a password.<br><br>Online twelve-step recovery meetings were attacked repeatedly through the same period. Intruders [displayed pornography, racial slurs, and antisemitic material](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/zoom-bombing-alcoholics-anonymous-meetings-coronavirus/), and in one case an attacker took control through screen sharing and returned each time he was removed. Access details reached them because groups had published meeting IDs on websites and social media so that anyone who needed a meeting could find one.<br><br>A threat can disrupt an event as effectively as an intrusion, and costs the actor less. GLAAD, working with the Anti-Defamation League's Center on Extremism, counted [161 protests and threats against drag events](https://glaad.org/anti-drag-report) in the United States between the start of 2022 and the end of March 2023, drawn from media reports across all fifty states and acknowledged as an undercount. Bomb threats closed an Indianapolis bookstore and emptied a Louisville venue in March 2023. A hoax threat disrupts an event whether or not a device exists, because the response to the threat is what clears the room. Swatting a livestream works the same way; a false emergency report brings [armed police to the broadcaster's address](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/technology-28970473) while the broadcast is running.<br><br><b>Establishing that an Event was Disrupted:</b><br>In June 2020 users on TikTok, joined by K-pop fan accounts, encouraged people to reserve tickets for Donald Trump's rally at the BOK Center in Tulsa with no intention of attending, so that seats would stand empty. Mary Jo Laupp's video making the suggestion [passed two million views](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2020-06-22/did-tiktok-users-kpop-fans-really-troll-donald-trump-tulsa-rally/12378768). The campaign claimed a million ticket requests for a venue seating around 19,000, and roughly 6,200 people attended. Brad Parscale said that registration amounted to an RSVP with a phone number, that the campaign filtered out false numbers, and that inflated requests never entered its planning. The pandemic is an obvious competing explanation for the turnout, and the reporting did not settle the question.<br><br>The attempt at deploying the Technique should be documented here, but the lack of impact should also be recorded. An analyst can record that a coordinated campaign acted on a scheduled occasion without recording that the occasion's attendance was reduced, and should do exactly that whenever the second claim is unsupported. Organisers have an incentive to attribute a poor event to sabotage, and disrupting parties have an incentive to claim credit for outcomes they did not cause. Neither claim is evidence of an impact.<br><br><b>Events which Fail without being Disrupted:</b><br>On 15 November 2022 Ticketmaster's presale for Taylor Swift's Eras Tour collapsed under demand, and two days later the [general public sale was cancelled](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/11/17/fridays-public-ticket-sale-for-taylor-swift-eras-tour-canceled-after-extreme-demand.html) for want of remaining inventory. Live Nation's chairman, Greg Maffei, said that around 1.5 million verified fans had been invited, that some 14 million people hit the site, and that demand would have filled 900 stadiums. Bot traffic was named as part of the problem. The sale did not function, and the audience it was built for could not get what it came for.<br><br>This is not an example of the Technique being deployed. The people who broke the sale were the people the sale existed for, doing the thing the sale existed for them to do. No participant acted against the occasion; its own capacity met genuine demand and lost. An operator's statement that bots were responsible is a claim about the presence of an actor, and an analyst has to test that claim rather than adopt it.
