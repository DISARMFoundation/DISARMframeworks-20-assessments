<td><a href="techniques/A1010.008.md">A1010.008</a></td>
<td>Subject Develops Informational Learned Helplessness</td>
<td>Summary: Activity has been assessed to have had the impact of inducing Informational Learned Helplessness, which is a state in which an individual believes they're unable to discern what information is true and what information is false, comes to accept this state as an unchangeable norm, and no longer attempts to tell the two apart.<br><br>Detail: In a paper titled “[Russian News Media, Digital Media, Informational Learned Helplessness, and Belief in COVID-19 Misinformation](https://academic.oup.com/ijpor/article/33/3/571/6294447)”, researchers Erik Nisbet and Olga Kamenchuk introduced the concept of Informational Learned Helplessness (ILH).<br><br>ILH is based on psychological research in which individuals who experience prolonged exposure to difficult or uncontrollable situations which they cannot avoid or remedy come to accept the situation as a given in what is called “learned helplessness”. This leads to the individual no longer attempting to correct or avoid the situation, as they believe there is no way their actions can alter the outcomes they experience.<br><br>Nisbet and Kamenchuk argue that “the high volume of competing claims and information inherent to infodemics increases cognitive and emotional costs of discerning accurate from inaccurate information for audiences”, which leads to ILH; an individual’s belief that they are unable to discern what information is true and what information is false, and their acceptance of this state as an unchangeable norm.</td>
<td>TA81</td>
</tr>

<td><a href="techniques/A1012.001.md">A1012.001</a></td>
<td>Subject Physically Injured</td>
<td>Summary: Activity has the assessed impact of causing bodily injury to the subject.<br><br>Detail: This impact records that the subject sustained bodily injury which an analyst assesses to be connected to the activity. <td><a href="techniques/A1012.002.md">A1012.002</a></td>
<td>Subject Subjected to Physical Sexual Violence</td>
<td>Summary: Activity has the assessed impact of causing the subject to be subjected to physical sexual violence.<br><br>Detail: This impact records that the subject was subjected to physical sexual violence which an analyst assesses to be connected to the activity.<br><br>Physical sexual violence can be assessed as an impact of an operation where the actor and the person who conducts the violence are different, the normal pattern in influence operations. An operation that publishes a target's home address does not itself commit physical sexual violence; assessing a resulting attack as an impact of the publication is an analytical claim which can be made.<br><br>Physical sexual violence does not require an identified assailant. Where a subject is attacked by an unidentified assailant, the impact is assessable even though no individual actor can be named.<br><br>Example<br><br>In December 2009 a woman in Casper, [Wyoming was raped at knifepoint in her home by Ty Oliver McDowell, a stranger. McDowell had answered a Craigslist advertisement placed by her former partner, Jebidiah James Stipe, who had posed as her, stated that she wished to act out a rape fantasy, and supplied her details.](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2010-jan-11-la-na-rape-craigslist11-2010jan11-story.html) McDowell said he had been duped into committing the offence.<br><br>Between October 2016 and March 2017 [Matthew Herrick was located by more than 1,000 men who arrived at his home and workplace expecting sex, at times sixteen in a day](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-grindr-app/grindr-defeats-appeal-over-harassment-on-gay-dating-app-idUSKCN1R81WD/). <td><a href="techniques/A1012.004.md">A1012.004</a></td>
<td>Subject Self-Harms</td>
<td>Summary: Activity has the assessed impact of contributing to the subject inflicting harm on themselves.<br><br>Detail: This impact records that the subject harmed themselves, and that an analyst assesses the activity to have contributed to that outcome. This definition specifies "contributory" rather than "causal", because self-harm has multiple causes and an assessment claiming sole causation would rarely be defensible.<br><br>Two types of material can induce self-harming behaviour. <td>Subject Dies by Suicide</td>
<td>Summary: Activity has the assessed impact of contributing to the subject's death by suicide.<br><br>Detail: This impact records that the subject died by suicide, and that an analyst assesses the activity to have contributed to that outcome.<br><br>One example of activity which brings about death by suicide is sextortion, in which an actor obtains a subject's intimate imagery and then demands payment under threat of distribution. <td><a href="techniques/A1012.006.md">A1012.006</a></td>
<td>Subject Killed</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A1012.007.md">A1012.007</a></td>
<td>Subject Dies</td>
<td>Summary: The subject has died in connection with or as a result of activity.<br><br>Detail: This impact records the death of the subject where an analyst assesses a connection to the activity.<br><br>The most significant case is death which no person directly caused (e.g. through physical attack). Health and vaccine disinformation can lead people to act on a false treatment claim, or to decline an immunisation on the strength of a fabricated risk. Deaths of this kind can occur at considerable scale and be attributable to an information environment rather than to any single act identifiable against an individual.<br><br>Death can also follow from conditions the activity produced. A subject who dies of untreated illness in detention, or who dies after losing access to medical care when their finances were cut off, died as a consequence of the activity without being directly killed.<br><br>The impact also covers deaths where attribution is not yet possible. Early reporting frequently establishes that a subject died in connection with activity before it establishes how, and recording the death should not require a premature claim about its cause. Where later evidence establishes the route, the assessment should be revised to the more specific impact.<br><br>Example<br><br>After COVID-19 reached Iran in early 2020, a rumour circulated that drinking alcohol prevented or cured infection. Iran's national coroner's authority recorded [728 deaths from alcohol poisoning between 20 February and 7 April 2020, against 66 in the equivalent period the previous year.](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/04/iran-700-dead-drinking-alcohol-cure-coronavirus-200427163529629.html) A later academic study put the fuller toll at [more than 6,150 poisonings and 804 deaths by 20 May 2020, with methanol ingested in the belief it protected against the virus, and some survivors left blind.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8111183/) In some regions, [alcohol poisoning mortality exceeded COVID-19 mortality over the same period.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8111183/)<br><br>A second pattern, in which disinformation suppresses immunisation and an outbreak follows, is documented in Samoa. UNICEF reported [that measles vaccination coverage in Samoa fell to 40 per cent for the first dose and 28 per cent for the second dose in 2018,](https://reliefweb.int/report/samoa/pacific-measles-outbreak-unicef-supporting-government-samoa-respond-outbreak) after the country's immunisation programme was suspended following the deaths of two infants shortly after vaccination in July that year. The World Health Organization said [the resulting crisis was driven in part by an anti-vaccine group's messaging on social media, which used the infants' deaths to discourage vaccination.](https://news.un.org/en/story/2019/12/1052801) Samoa's Ministry of Health reported a [cumulative total of 5,707 confirmed measles cases and 83 deaths by 20 January 2020, in a population of about 200,000.](https://reliefweb.int/report/samoa/health-emergency-operation-centre-update-measles-outbreak-january-20-2020)</td>

<td>Subject Physically Detained</td>
<td>Summary: The subject has been deprived of liberty and held in custody in connection with or as a result of activity.<br><br>Detail: This impact records that the subject was taken into custody. It covers detention by state authorities with or without legal process, imprisonment following conviction, and detention by non-state actors.<br><br>Custody is the defining feature. A subject who is prevented from leaving a country, whose passport is confiscated, or who is placed under a bail condition has had their freedom restricted without being taken into custody, and that is a different outcome recorded elsewhere.<br><br>The detained person is often not the person an operation is aimed at. Detention of relatives is a standard coercion by proxy mechanism, and the detention of a journalist's family in their country of origin is an impact of a campaign directed at the journalist abroad. Recording it requires attributing an outcome suffered by one person to activity targeting another.<br><br>Detention rarely concludes a sequence. Loss of employment, financial loss, deterioration in health, and damage to the subject's relationships follow from it routinely, and each is a separate outcome in its own right.<br><br>Example<br><br>Gulchehra Hoja is a Uyghur journalist with Radio Free Asia's Uyghur Service who reports on detention in Xinjiang. [On 28 January 2018 she published an interview with a survivor of the internment camps, and three days later, Chinese authorities detained twenty-four more of her relatives.](https://focustaiwan.tw/culture/202607210008) [Her brother had previously been arrested in 2017 and spent three years in an internment camp; her mother was detained in 2018 and held for two months.](https://hrf.org/latest/chinese-communist-party-stop-coercing-family-of-uyghur-journalist-gulchehra-hoja/) Her parents have repeatedly been refused passports in retaliation for her work, and [Chinese authorities published a video in which her mother and brother were coerced into stating on camera that they were living normal lives.](https://hrf.org/latest/chinese-communist-party-stop-coercing-family-of-uyghur-journalist-gulchehra-hoja/) Rights groups, including Amnesty International, separately documented around [twenty relatives detained in the same period, some over contact with Hoja in a WeChat group, and condemned the pattern as retaliation against her reporting.](https://www.rfa.org/english/news/uyghur/reporters-relatives-03012018164751.html)</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A1012.009.md">A1012.009</a></td>
<td>Subject Forced to Relocate</td>
<td>Summary: The subject has left their home, and in some cases their country, because of activity directed at them or at someone connected to them.<br><br>Detail: This impact records that the subject changed where they live as a direct response to activity. It covers a single move made out of fear for safety, repeated moves following successive exposures of a new address, and departure from a country altogether.<br><br>The move itself, not the activity that caused it, is the defining feature. Doxxing, swatting, a demonstrated capability to locate someone, and a credible threat of physical harm are all mechanisms that can produce this impact, and the specific mechanism should be recorded separately using the relevant Observation or Technique. What this item captures is that the subject changed address because of it.<br><br>Relocation is often incomplete as a remedy. A subject who moves after being doxxed can be doxxed again at the new address, and each successive exposure can trigger a further move. Recording a single instance of this impact does not imply the matter is resolved; a pattern of repeated relocation is itself evidence of an actor's continued capability and intent, and should be recorded as such rather than folded into a single tagging.<br><br>Relocation carries its own downstream costs. Loss of employment, financial loss, disruption to a child's schooling, and loss of a support network are common consequences and are each a separate outcome to be recorded in their own right, not absorbed into this item.<br><br>Example<br><br>Li Ying, known online as Teacher Li, is a [Chinese dissident based in Italy who relays uncensored information and images from contacts inside China to a large following on X.](https://safeguarddefenders.com/en/blog/teacher-li-full-transnational-repression-story) His identity was first doxxed by anonymous accounts on 28 November 2022, and his home address in Italy has been leaked online more than once since. As a precaution, he listed an old address rather than his current one on a passport renewal request in February 2023, but an individual linked to pro-Beijing community associations in Milan traced that old address through a former roommate the following month. That address and his phone number were then published on WeChat, after which he began changing where he lived repeatedly out of fear for his safety.</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A1013.001.md">A1013.001</a></td>
<td>Subject's Family Relationships Damaged</td>
<td>Summary: Activity has the assessed impact of damaging the subject's relationships with family members.<br><br>Detail: This impact records damage to the subject's relationships with family members. It covers a range of damage from strained contact through to permanent severance, and does not require that any relationship explicitly ended.<br><br>There are several different ways in which this impact can arise. <td><a href="techniques/A2001.002.md">A2001.002</a></td>
<td>Crowd Out Contributions to an Information Space</td>
<td><b>Summary:</b><br><i>Crowd Out Contributions to an Information Space</i> describes the deliberate saturation of a specific, bounded space with content, so that it can no longer effectively carry the information it otherwise would. It is not the same as producing a large volume of content; an actor is only crowding out contributions if a particular information space was saturated.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>For the purposes of this Technique, an information space is defined as any information ecosystem where an individual could reasonably expect to access a collection of information on a given topic, theme, or collection of topics. Public information spaces include the material carried alongside a hashtag, the comments on a post or article, the chat accompanying a live stream, and a user's feed or timeline. Private information spaces include a group chat, a person's direct messages, and an email inbox. Identifying the affected space is therefore a precondition for identifying this Technique; an actor producing a large volume of content is not crowding out contributions unless the analyst can name the space that content has saturated.<br><br>Contributions to an information space are crowded out when the space receives an influx of content exceeding the levels of activity typical for that space, displacing the content it would otherwise carry. A space's average activity changes what qualifies; a group chat could be saturated by 100 messages, where the same activity wouldn't register as abnormal on a busy hashtag. However, crowding out requires a plurality of content. A single contribution which renders a space unusable is disruption of a different kind.<br><br>Where a space has an observable history, crowding out can be identified by comparing activity against average metrics for a comparable timeframe. Where a space has no history, either because it is private or because the space was created by the activity itself, as when an actor occupies a hashtag previously unused, the comparison is against what the space would reasonably be expected to carry rather than against a measured baseline.<br><br>An information space being saturated is not by itself an indicator of malicious behaviour. There can be a naturally occurring spike in activity in response to events, such as a mass of anguished posting after conceding a goal in the final minutes of a World Cup match. This surge of posts can have the impact of making it harder to find any other topic on one's timeline. What makes crowding out a Technique is that the volume of content is the intention of the behaviour, rather than emerging from organic activity. In an organic surge, individual participants post for their own reasons and the aggregate effect is a byproduct of many independent decisions; in crowding out, an actor posts in order to occupy a space, whether by coordinating many assets posting in near-simultaneity or by repeatedly publishing from a single asset.<br><br>Because volume is the important element of crowding out, actors don't need to curate a persona or craft a narrative to achieve their effect. Content used to crowd out contributions is frequently duplicated or near-duplicated; [research on political WhatsApp groups in Brazil](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/31354) characterises what its authors call flooding attacks as the sharing of large numbers of usually duplicate messages within a short window. Content is also frequently irrelevant to the space it occupies, since relevance is not required for displacement; in the WhatsApp study, 62% of these attacks were carried out using stickers, content carrying almost no semantic payload. Where the objective is to alter the meaning of a space rather than merely obstruct it, content is instead matched to the target, as in [the saturation of the #DoctorsSpeakUp vaccination campaign hashtag with anti-vaccine material](https://www.newsweek.com/doctors-speak-twitter-trend-anti-vaxxers-vaccine-hashtag-1490692).<br><br><b>Crowding Out Contributions as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>[A study of 19 million messages across roughly 1,600 Brazilian public political WhatsApp groups](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/31354) identified these attacks in approximately 7% of groups, defined as an attacker sharing a large number of usually duplicate messages within a short period, with most attacks resolving in under four minutes. This qualifies as crowding out contributions to an information space; the space is tightly bounded, being a single group with a small membership and a linear message feed, so its carrying capacity is low and easily exceeded. The volume is intended rather than organically occurring, since the messages are duplicates published by an attacker rather than independent contributions by members. And the content was not the payload: 62% of the attacks were carried out using stickers, which convey almost nothing. Nobody was being argued with; their information space was being attacked. The suppressive effect follows from the mechanism rather than from any message. Nothing was deleted, no member was removed, and the group continued to exist; what stopped was the group's usability. Members could not follow a conversation, locate earlier messages, or coordinate, because the feed had been consumed.<br><br>Crowding out can suppress without the displacing content being noise. In March 2020 the physician Zubin Damania launched #DoctorsSpeakUp to coordinate doctors posting publicly in support of vaccination. Anti-vaccine accounts adopted the hashtag in volume, and research found that [the majority of tweets carrying it were oriented against vaccines](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10410236.2022.2058159), with fewer than 17% coming from pro-vaccine doctors who identified themselves as such. The mechanism is the same as in the WhatsApp groups: intended volume into a bounded space, exceeding what that space would ordinarily carry. What differs is the composition of the displacing content, and therefore what followed. While the WhatsApp groups lost functionality as an information space, the hashtag kept functioning, but the message associated with it had been reversed. The pro-vaccine doctors were not only displaced from the space they had created; the space now argued against them.<br><br>Crowding out is not limited to spaces where people talk to each other. The US Federal Communications Commission's 2017 public consultation on repealing net neutrality received roughly 22 million comments, orders of magnitude above what such a consultation ordinarily receives. [An investigation found that around 18 million of them were fraudulent](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/reports/oag-fakecommentsreport.pdf), including 8.5 million opposing net neutrality submitted through a $4.2 million campaign funded by the broadband industry, which used the identities of real people without their consent. The fraud was not one-sided; the same investigation found that a single 19-year-old submitted over 7.7 million pro-net-neutrality comments using fabricated identities. The consultation record is a bounded information space; it is the permanent, publicly accessible collection of responses to a defined question, relied on by the agency at the time and by any court reviewing the decision afterwards. As with the hashtag, it continued to function; what it stopped doing was carrying the views of the people who had genuinely submitted them, which survived only as a small minority of a record overwhelmingly composed of fabrications, while some real people found their names attached to positions they had never held. The case also demonstrates that this Technique does not require a network of collaborating individuals, since 7.7 million of those submissions came from one person.<br><br><b>Crowding Out Contributions as a Method of Harassment:</b><br>Twitch hate raids are an example of deploying <i>Crowd Out Contributions to an Information Space</i> as a form of harassment. From mid-2021, automated accounts filled individual streamers' live chats with racist, misogynist, and antisemitic content while they were broadcasting. Twitch [sued two users](https://techcrunch.com/2021/09/13/twitch-hate-raids-lawsuits/) in September 2021, alleging that one of them alone was linked to around 3,000 bot accounts, and the practice has since been studied in two CSCW papers ([Cai et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16248); [Han et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.03946)). The space is a single channel's live chat, which is easily saturated since it is an unthreaded scrolling window displaying ten to twenty messages with no search and no persistence. Displacement is therefore total and immediate at volumes that would have less impact in other information spaces.<br><br>The harassment element is compounded by the volume of abuse. The target cannot moderate at the rate of arrival, so the ordinary defensive tools, if any are available, fail as a countermeasure. The target's own community is displaced along with the abuse, so the harm is not only to the individual but to the space they built and the relationships in it. And the abuse is public and synchronous, witnessed live by whoever remained, which exacerbates the reputational and emotional dimensions. The same dynamic appears in individual rather than broadcast spaces, as when [Maria Ressa reported receiving an average of 90 hate messages per hour](https://gijn.org/stories/online-harassment-lessons-from-the-philippines/) for a month after Rappler published its investigation into networked political trolling in the Philippines.<br><br><b>Contributions Crowded Out Organically:</b><br>On 2 June 2020, during the protests following the killing of George Floyd, millions of users posted plain black squares to Instagram as part of Blackout Tuesday, an initiative conceived by the music industry figures Jamila Thomas and Brianna Agyemang. A large proportion tagged those posts #BlackLivesMatter and #BLM, and the tags were saturated within hours. The material displaced was operational: protest logistics, safety guidance, bail fund details, contacts for lawyers offering pro bono representation, and video documentation of police conduct. [Organisers publicly asked participants to strip the BLM tags from their posts and to use #BlackoutTuesday or #TheShowMustBePaused instead](https://time.com/5846514/blackout-tuesday-screen/). On every observable measure contributions to this information space were crowded out: the space was bounded, the volume vastly exceeded anything the tag would ordinarily carry, and content that people had a reasonable expectation of finding there became unreachable at the moment it was most needed.<br><br>This example demonstrates that the impact of behaviour is not what defines Technique usage. The volume was emergent rather than intended. Each participant posted once, sincerely, in support of the movement whose information they buried, and nobody was acting in order to occupy the space. The correction is itself evidence of this; participants removed the tags once the effect was explained to them, and an actor crowding out contributions deliberately does not stop on being told that it is working.<br><br><b>Differentiating Crowding Out Contributions from Astroturfing:</b><br>Crowding out and astroturfing are often conflated, but they are separated by what the volume of messaging is for. [Astroturfing is conventionally defined](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10584609.2019.1661888) as a centrally coordinated campaign in which participants pretend to be ordinary citizens acting independently, concealing the sponsor so that an orchestrated message appears to be spontaneous public sentiment. In astroturfing, the volume of messaging is evidence to support the claim that many independent people hold this view. In crowding out, the volume of messaging occupies a space, and nothing about it needs to be believed.<br><br>One way to differentiate between astroturfing and crowding out is whether public attribution of activity neutralises the behaviour. Astroturfing is less effective once exposed, because credibility of the messaging is important to its success. Contributions to an information space continue to be crowded out after everyone knows what is happening, which is why the [Brazilian political WhatsApp groups](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/31354) remained unusable whether or not their members understood that the stickers were an attack.<br><br>A second differentiator is the minimum volume each requires. Astroturfing has no volume floor; a small number of accounts posting considered content while presenting as unconnected individuals is astroturfing and nothing else. Crowding out has a minimum volume, since it is defined by exceeding a space's capacity. Volume is therefore a requirement of crowding out, and merely an element of some astroturfing campaigns.<br><br>The behaviours are not mutually exclusive. An incident may be both crowding out and astroturfing. The FCC consultation is the clearest instance in this entry: submitting 8.5 million responses in the names of real people manufactures the appearance of independent public support, which is astroturfing, while 18 million fraudulent submissions overwhelming a 22 million response record is crowding out.</td>

<td>Coercion by Proxy</td>
<td>[Coercion by Proxy](https://freedomhouse.org/report/special-report/2020/home-and-abroad-coercion-proxy-tool-transnational-repression) refers to attempts to control a target through harming or threatening harm to individuals or institutions that the target cares for.<br><br>Instead of attacking a target directly, an actor harms or threatens to harm people the target has a relationship with (family members, colleagues, employers, schools, religious or civic institutions) most often with the goal of coercing, silencing, or punishing the target by proxy.<br><br>Sometimes referred to as “collective punishment”, authoritarian governments operating within their own territory use Coercion by Proxy to target the relatives, associates or institutions connected to critics living or working abroad, using domestic repression as a means of controlling people the state cannot otherwise reach. [Human Rights Watch's global review of how governments repress nationals abroad](https://www.hrw.org/report/2024/02/22/we-will-find-you/global-look-how-governments-repress-nationals-abroad) lists collective punishment of relatives as one of the core recurring methods of transnational repression.<br><br>Coercion by Proxy as a technique is best understood seen through the lens of the chilling effect; the mere possibility of punishment, independent of whether it is ever carried out, is enough to make people restrict their own behaviour out of fear and uncertainty. This matters for Coercion by Proxy specifically because the technique's deterrent power doesn't depend on the original target being harmed at all. A credible threat, or even a single demonstrated case against someone else's family, can chill an entire network of potential sources, journalists or activists who simply witness it.</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A3004.001.md">A3004.001</a></td>
<td>Harass</td>
<td><b>Summary:</b><br><i>Harass</i> describes a participant acting on an identifiable target, where either the participant sought to alarm, distress, frighten, or constrain that target, or a reasonable person knowing what the participant knew, and taking the target's position into consideration, would have understood the conduct to do so. It is carried out online, offline, or across both. Harassment can be achieved through a wide variety of methods, and as such identifying the use of this Technique is not about focusing on a single mechanism. Instead, <i>Harass</i> is defined by an identifiable target of activity, and that the activity goes beyond annoyance or trivial upset for a person in that target's position.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>Harassment has two criteria; targeting, and intent to harass, or a reasonable understanding of behaviour as harassment.<br><br><b>Targeting:</b> There was a specific target of harassment (a person, a group, an institution), and a participant acted on that target. Identifying the target is a precondition for applying the Technique; for harassment to occur, a target must have been harassed. A participant who publishes discriminatory content about no one in particular has not taken part in direct harassment, but rather a form of hate speech. A participant who publishes the same content about or sent to an identifiable person has.<br><br><b>Intent to harass, or reasonable understanding of behaviour as harassment:</b> Either the actor sought to alarm, distress, frighten, or constrain the target, or a "reasonable person" knowing what the actor knew, and taking into consideration the position of the target, would have understood the conduct to do so.<br><br>The reason that harassment cannot be defined alone by an actor's intent to harass is because of cases where an actor believes their behaviour to be unproblematic, such as a stalker who perceives their unwanted attention as an act of devotion. Similarly, the reason we cannot dismiss intent entirely is for cases where an actor fails to make their target feel alarmed, distressed, frightened or constrained, despite their real intent to do so. [The UK's 1997 Protection from Harassment Act](https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1997/40/section/1) is built the same way, prohibiting conduct the actor "knows or ought to know" amounts to harassment.<br><br>To assess harassment from the perspective of a "reasonable person", an analyst has to first settle two things.<br><br><b>What did the actor know?</b> This is not about assessing the actor's intent, but whether with the information available to them "I did not realise this could be considered harassment" is a viable explanation for their behaviour.<br><br>For example, consider a stalker who knows they have sent hundreds of messages, knows the subject has blocked them repeatedly, and knows they have moved house. A reasonable person holding those facts concludes that the subject is frightened of them, but a stalker may decide that the subject is playing hard to get. They had the information but declined the reasonable inference, and this check takes lack of awareness away as a defence. It cuts the other way too; someone who mentions having seen an individual at a supermarket, with no way of knowing that that individual was in hiding at the time and could be distressed by this information. The 'actor' in this case is judged on what they knew, and being unaware the individual was in hiding, could reasonably have not intended their comment to have been upsetting.<br><br><b>What is the position of the target?</b> Taking on the position of the subject of activity helps raise awareness of cultural or demographic considerations which could make behaviour which seems trivial to a "reasonable person" in one setting seem distressing to a "reasonable person" in another.<br><br>[Interviews conducted with 199 women in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh found that what counts as abusive material there differs from the Western picture.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3290605.3300232) Fully clothed photographs were considered to be threatening artefacts, where in the West it is commonly only explicit images that get reported. Examples of abuse included a participant whose account was altered to show her falsely engaged to a man she did not know, and impersonation used to humiliate women seen to have departed from local norms, including by wearing modern clothing. An NGO helpline worker in Pakistan stated that "a fully-clothed photo can lead to suicide here in conservative regions of Pakistan."<br><br>Taking an example of an AI-generated picture of a woman sitting with a man, or with her head uncovered. To an analyst who doesn't consider the cultural perspective of the target it may appear unremarkable, and the study referenced above records platforms reaching the same conclusion, declining to act because fully clothed photographs break no terms of service. However, to a person in the target's position it can lead to loss of employment, marriage prospects, and physical safety. In order to appreciate the potential for harm from such cases, analysts are asked to take into consideration a person in the target's position.<br><br>A target's failure to be alarmed, distressed, frightened, or constrained does not mean Harassment has not occurred. An analyst needs to consider the potential for distress as a result of activity for the average person in the target's position. A target who happened to be feeling resilient at the time of their harassment, or who had a higher threshold to begin with, has still been harassed, even if activity did not have a lasting impact on them.<br><br>Harassment can be conducted directly, such as an actor sending a threatening or abusive message, or it can be conducted indirectly, such as by delivering material about the target to an audience, as with a post that publishes the target's home address. Harassment can also be conducted via a third party, such as by submitting a false report to a platform, or making a hoax emergency call.<br><br>A wide variety of behaviour can be considered Harassment as long as it meets the criteria outlined above. This includes other Techniques in the DISARM Framework which aren't under the Harassment category. For example, Crowding Out Contributions to an Information Space can be done with the goal of distressing those running or using that space. If the tests for Harassment are met, then an analyst can consider the Technique as having been used as a method of Harassment.<br><br><b>Harassment as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>In early October 2016 the Philippine news site Rappler published a three-part series on social media propaganda in the country. Its chief executive Maria Ressa has [written that she then received an average of 90 hate messages per hour for the next month](https://time.com/5505458/facebook-maria-ressa-philippines/), with the attacks running to her appearance, her voice, and threats of rape and murder. Ressa and her colleagues named the pattern patriotic trolling, which an international research coalition defined as [state-sponsored online hate and harassment campaigns used to silence and intimidate individuals](https://www.canada.ca/en/security-intelligence-service/corporate/publications/who-said-what-the-security-challenges-of-modern-disinformation/chapter-8-from-likes-to-leaders-the-impact-of-social-networks-in-the-philippines.html).<br><br>Non-state movements run the same behaviour against the same kind of target. Meta's first Adversarial Threat Report, published on 1 December 2021, [announced the removal of a network originating in Italy and France which targeted medical professionals, journalists, and elected officials with mass harassment](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/12/metas-adversarial-threat-report/), using a combination of authentic, duplicate, and fake accounts to mass comment on posts in order to intimidate the people behind them and suppress their views. Meta linked the network to the anti-vaccination movement V_V, and its head of emerging harms investigations Mike Dvilyanski said [the campaign used Telegram to train recruits and to circulate word of who to target](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20211201-facebook-hits-anti-vaccine-campaign-harassing-doctors).<br><br>Interviews conducted with seventeen academics, journalists, and activists who had been harassed for their work found participants who [changed research agenda after harassment, left institutions, and would have left the academy altogether without support](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11145). One professor reported first-rank doctoral students quitting the programme because they were not up to the battle. The study records the emotional cost alongside the professional one, with several participants crying during their interviews, three reporting that the FBI had warned them of a threat to their life, and one describing lasting rage at senior colleagues who watched the campaign and said nothing in public. It also records the harm spreading past the target, since harassment arrived in departmental administrators' inboxes, faculty ran contentious projects alone rather than expose collaborators, and institutions with no policy asked the harassed person to write one.<br><br><b>Potential Impacts:</b><br>Impacts are organised by affected party. An item may appear under more than one party where the justification differs.<br><br><b>Primary Target:</b><br><b>A1001.001: Target of Activity Defamed.</b> Harassment campaigns routinely carry fabricated accusations, as the operation against Teacher Li did in circulating the claim that he had defrauded a woman.<br><b>A1005.006: Activity Exhausts Subject's Resources.</b> Replacing devices and buying physical security cost money that would otherwise go to the target's own work.<br><b>A1012.009: Subject Forced to Relocate.</b> Doxxing makes relocation the direct consequence of the act, since each newly exposed address has to be abandoned.<br><b>A1012.006: Subject Killed.</b> False emergency reports have produced fatal responses, and [Thomas et al.](https://rist.tech.cornell.edu/papers/sok-abuse.pdf) record one swatting incident in 2017 which killed the person at the address.<br><b>A1008.005: Feeling of Anger Evoked in Subject.</b> Anger is a common response where the target regards the attack as unjust.<br><b>A1008.006: Feeling of Anxiety Evoked in Subject.</b> Uncertainty about what has been exposed and what will follow produces a sustained background state rather than a single reaction, which is why Teacher Li began moving frequently once his address circulated.<br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> Fear is the intended product of a threat, and three of the researchers interviewed had been warned by the FBI of a threat to their life.<br><b>A1008.018: Feeling of Horror Evoked in Subject.</b> Applies where the material is sexualised or violent, as with the sexual imagery of Teacher Li's parents.<br><b>A1008.024: Feeling of Sadness Evoked in Subject.</b> Several of the researchers interviewed wept during the interview and described being unable to avoid internalising the hatred.<br><b>A1010.001: Subject Disengages from Information Ecosystem.</b> Targets stop posting or broadcasting until an attack ends, which removes them from the medium their work depends on.<br><b>A1010.003: Subject Disengages from Cause.</b> Full withdrawal from a line of work follows where the harassment is tied to that work specifically.<br><b>A1010.005: Subject Ceases Sharing Information.</b> Targets stop publishing, which is the outcome the suppression cases are built to produce.<br><b>A1010.006: Subject Self-Censors when Sharing Information.</b> Researchers interviewed described avoiding contentious topics and waiting for tenure before undertaking work they expected to attract attacks.<br><b>A1011.002: Activity Restricts Subject's Freedom of Expression.</b> False reports submitted to a platform can get a target's account suspended, which restricts expression without any prohibition.<br><b>A1011.004: Activity Restricts Subject's Academic Freedom.</b> Applies where the target is a researcher and the work curtailed is research, such as abandoned projects and rejected grant applications.<br><b>A1011.005: Activity Restricts Subject's Media Freedom.</b> Applies where the target is a journalist and the reporting is what stops.<br><br><b>People Connected to the Target:</b><br><b>A1008.014: Feeling of Empathic Pain Evoked in Subject.</b> Targets suffer through watching harassment of people they care about, which is the effect the sexual imagery of Teacher Li's parents was calculated to produce in him.<br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> Relatives reached directly experience fear in their own right rather than by proxy, as Teacher Li's parents did under daily visits from security officials.<br><br><b>Employer, Host, or Platform Community:</b><br><b>A1005.006: Activity Exhausts Subject's Resources.</b> Institutions carry the cost of responding.<br><b>A1001.002: Target of Activity Undermined.</b> An institution which fails to protect a target is shown publicly to be unable to do so.<br><br><b>Wider Audience:</b><br><b>A1010.004: Subject Declines to Start Sharing Information.</b> People who have not yet spoken decide not to begin. [Safeguard Defenders describes the climate of fear the Chinese Communist Party wishes to create in concerned communities inside and outside China](https://safeguarddefenders.com/en/blog/teacher-li-full-transnational-repression-story).<br><b>A1010.006: Subject Self-Censors when Sharing Information.</b> Witnesses moderate their own output without ever being targeted themselves.<br><b>A1007.002: Activity Dissuades Action.</b> Demonstrative harassment is aimed at the people watching, and the followers who unfollowed Teacher Li after being questioned by public security officers took exactly the action they were asked to take.<br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> Members of a targeted category recognise that they could be selected next, which is the mechanism by which category-level targeting reaches people who were never contacted.<br><b>A1009.001: Spread Hate.</b> Racist, misogynist, and antisemitic material delivered into a live chat reaches everyone watching the broadcast, not only the streamer it was aimed at.<br><b>A1009.002: Degrade Quality of Information Ecosystem.</b> The aggregate of many people individually withdrawing or self-censoring reduces what is available to everyone.

<td>Harass en Masse</td>
<td><b>Summary:</b><br>Harass en Masse describes more than one asset or individual acting on one target close enough together that the target receives what they send as a mass rather than as a sequence. What identifies the Technique is not who built the mass but that a mass arrived. Examples include a raid organised on a private channel with a target list and shared tooling, an audience acting on a name its amplifier gave them, and a crowd that converges on a target with nobody having organised anything. In Harass en Masse the volume is part of the harm; behaviour which may otherwise be brushed off can be overwhelming when received in plurality over a short period of time.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>Harass en Masse is one form of harassment, and an incident has to satisfy four conditions before it is recorded as this Technique.<br><br><b>Targeting:</b> There was a specific target of harassment (a person, a group, an institution), and a participant acted on that target. Identifying the target is a precondition for applying the Technique; for harassment to occur, a target must have been harassed.<br><br><b>Intent to harass, or reasonable understanding of the culmination of behaviour as harassment:</b> Either the actor sought to alarm, distress, frighten, or constrain the target, or a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew, and taking into consideration the position of the target, would have understood the conduct to do so. For more information about both the "intent to harass" and "reasonable understanding of behaviour as harassment" paths, see <b>A3004.001: Harass</b>.<br><br>Note that when looking at behaviour conducted as part of mass harassment, the bar for what is considered to be harassing is lower, as the mass of abuse amplifies the harm experienced. Where one message alone may not be considered harassment, when seen as part of a wave of abuse, it can hit the bar for being considered harassment.<br><br><b>Plurality:</b> More than one asset or individual acted on the target. For example, a group of individuals collaborating to harass a target, or a single actor using automation to send many bot accounts at a target.<br><br><b>Convergence in time:</b> The contributions arrived close enough together that the target received them as a mass rather than as a sequence. The comparison is against what the target could reasonably have dealt with, not against a fixed number or a rule of thumb about the channel: a streamer with a moderation team can absorb a rush of messages that would overwhelm one without, and the same message rate is routine in one chat and unmanageable in another.<br><br>Coordination of the harassment is not a condition of this Technique. Whether an actor manufactured mass harassment is a judgement about actors rather than about what happened to the target, and it is recorded separately. The reason is that an analyst is much more likely to be able to confirm that harassment en masse has occurred than to confirm that it was explicitly coordinated. Separating coordination from this Technique lets analysts document harassment en masse without also confirming whether it was coordinated.<br><br><b>Different Ways a Mass Forms:</b><br>A mass forms four ways. All four are in scope, and which one applies is recorded as actor analysis rather than as a condition of the Technique.<br><br><b>Group agreement.</b> Actors within an existing group arrange the harassment campaign between themselves. There is a channel, a list, a piece of tooling, or a thread where the target is posted, and the group collaborates to harass the target.<br><br>Meta describes this behaviour as "brigading", defining it as [people working together to mass comment, mass post, or engage in other repetitive mass behaviours to harass or silence others](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/12/metas-adversarial-threat-report/). In their example, a network it removed, linked to the anti-vaccination movement V_V in Italy and France targeted medical professionals, journalists, and elected officials, and Graphika described the group as [using Telegram to train recruits and to circulate word of who to target](https://www.graphika.com/reports/viral-vendetta).<br><br><b>Actors controlling multiple assets.</b> One operator provides the plurality using technology such as automation. A single actor, or several coordinating actors who each control multiple assets, deploy a harassment campaign which appears to be a vast wave of harassment coming from a wider pool of collaborators than actually originated the attack.<br><br>For example, Twitch alleged in its September 2021 complaint that [one defendant alone was linked to around 3,000 bot accounts deployed in hate raids](https://techcrunch.com/2021/09/13/twitch-hate-raids-lawsuits/), and that the defendants altered their code to evade detection.<br><br><b>Recruitment.</b> An actor assembles people who need not know each other, by encouraging an audience to act on a target. For example, the pattern in which a user [posts a link to a YouTube video on 4chan's /pol/, actors arrive together, and raiders return afterwards to report what they did](https://seclab.bu.edu/people/gianluca/papers/youtube-CSCW2019.pdf). There is no membership and no list, only a thread and a convention.<br><br>An actor who names a target but does not explicitly ask their followers to harass them is explored further in the Technique <b>A3004.004: Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</b>.<br><br><b>Emergence.</b> Nobody assembles the mass. Researchers named mass harassment arising without an organiser as an [online firestorm](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13527266.2013.797778), a sudden discharge of large quantities of negative messages against a person, company, or group, often expressing indignation without pointing to a specific criticism. Nothing in that description requires an organiser, and in a firestorm there usually is not one.<br><br><b>Harassing en Masse as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>The V_V network is the case where mass harassment is deployed with the goal of suppressing unwanted information. Graphika described the group as [using Telegram to train recruits and to circulate word of who to target](https://www.graphika.com/reports/viral-vendetta), directing them at medical professionals, journalists, and elected officials, providing direct links to their posts with templated harassment ready to paste in. Their targets' profiles were flooded with harassment including swastikas, and accusations of genocide, overwhelming the conversation and dissuading users from sharing accurate public health information.<br><br><b>Potential Impacts:</b><br>Impacts are organised by affected party. An item may appear under more than one party where the justification differs.<br><br><b>Primary Target:</b><br><b>A1010.005: Subject Ceases Sharing Information.</b> Twitch pleaded that some streamers stopped broadcasting until an attack ended.<br><b>A1010.006: Subject Self-Censors when Sharing Information.</b> Targets avoid the subjects that drew a mob, which is cheaper than absorbing another one.<br><b>A1010.007: Subject's Audience Disengages with Information Shared by Subject.</b> A chat or reply thread filled faster than it can be read displaces the target's own community along with the abuse.<br><b>A1011.002: Activity Restricts Subject's Freedom of Expression.</b> Being unable to be heard through the volume is a restriction achieved without any prohibition.<br><b>A1008.006: Feeling of Anxiety Evoked in Subject.</b> [Cai and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16248) record psychological harm as one of the harms hate raids cause marginalised streamers.<br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> They record safety threat as another, since a mob contains contributions a single harasser would not have supplied.<br><br><b>The Target's Community and Bystanders:</b><br><b>A1010.002: Subject Disengages from Community.</b> Community loss is another harm Cai and colleagues identify, and it falls on the people who were in the room rather than on the person who was targeted.<br><b>A1009.002: Degrade Quality of Information Ecosystem.</b> [Kumar and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.03697) find that conflicts reduce the overall activity of users in targeted communities over the long term.<br><br><b>Wider Audience:</b><br><b>A1010.004: Subject Declines to Start Sharing Information.</b> A mob is a public event by construction, and the people watching draw the intended conclusion.<br><b>A1007.002: Activity Dissuades Action.</b> Deterring bystanders is usually the point of a demonstrative pile-on rather than a side effect of it.<br><b>A1009.001: Spread Hate.</b> Abusive and discriminatory material delivered into a shared space reaches everyone present.</td>

<td>Dox</td>
<td><b>Summary:</b><br><i>Dox</i> describes an actor publishing information about a target which makes them findable, attaches their identity to something they did anonymously, or discloses a private fact whose disclosure carries consequences for them. It covers a home address posted to a forum, a pseudonymous account matched to a legal name, and a person's relationship history circulated in a community where it will cost them. What identifies the Technique is that the actor moved information into a setting the target had kept it out of, rather than saying anything about them.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>Doxxing is one form of harassment, and an incident has to satisfy three conditions before it is recorded as this Technique.<br><br><b>A target.</b> There is a target, and it is a person, a group of people, or an institution. The target is identified by the actor in some way to an audience. The target doesn't require being explicitly named as long as they are identifiable to the audience.<br><br><b>Publication of one of three kinds of information.</b> The actor published or transmitted information about the target to people who did not hold it, and that information is in [at least one of the following forms](https://projects.itforchange.net/online-violence-gender-and-law-guide/module-2-typologies-of-online-gender-based-offenses-in-law/2-8-doxxing/):<br><br><b>Targeting Doxxing:</b> Publishing information which makes the target findable or contactable. Associating with a target a home address, a telephone number, an employer, a school, a vehicle registration, a daily routine, or a photograph of a front door are each examples of ways that a target can be made more findable or contactable.<br><b>Deanonymising Doxxing:</b> Publishing information which attaches the target's identity to something they did anonymously or under a pseudonym, such as a legal name matched to a previously unassociated online account.<br><b>Delegitimising Doxxing:</b> Publishing information which discloses private facts about the target's life whose disclosure carries consequences for a person in their position. Examples include sexual history, relationship or marital status, health, immigration status, religious practice, and the identities of family members. Which of these carries consequences depends on where the target lives, who they are, and factors unique to their culture. An analyst who considers doxxing to only cover information like addresses and telephone numbers can miss this type of doxxing.<br><br><b>Intent to harass, or reasonable understanding of the publication as harassment.</b> Either the actor sought to alarm, distress, frighten, or constrain the target, or a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew, and taking into consideration the position of the target, would have understood the publication to do so. For more information about both the "intent to harass" and "reasonable understanding of behaviour as harassment" paths, see <b>A3004.001: Harass</b>.<br><br>Doxxes do not need to publish information which was previously a secret. Information gathered from open sources and published to an audience who did not previously hold that information can be considered doxxing even though a motivated actor from the same audience could find such information themselves. To identify doxxing [analysts should consider whether something shared somewhere expected, intentional, or desired has been moved somewhere unexpected, unintentional, and undesired.](https://www.nomos-elibrary.de/10.5771/1615-634X-2020-3-273.pdf)<br><br>Doxxing occurred through open source applications known as Sulli Deals and Bulli Bai, hosted on GitHub, which put Indian Muslim women up for mock auction using [photographs sourced from the women's own social media handles without their consent](https://projects.itforchange.net/online-violence-gender-and-law-guide/module-2-typologies-of-online-gender-based-offenses-in-law/2-8-doxxing/). While the information was available on an open source, the women did not intend for the information to be collated and shared to an audience of men on a mock auction website, and its publication there made them identifiable to an audience which they did not intend their information to be provided to.<br><br>The principle is that publishing information in one setting is not consent to its appearing in every setting. Where someone shares information about themselves with a person or group online, they retain the right to control the flow of information pertaining to them. This is already how DISARM defines the Observation <b>T4202.007: Content Constitutes Leak of Private Material</b>; private materials having been non-consensually published to an audience not originally intended, which is a contextual-integrity test rather than a secrecy test. An analyst applying this Technique should read it that way.<br><br>Assessing the context of informational flow helps analysts see that the same information carries different consequences for different people. What may appropriately be revealed about a person depends on the social and political context they live in, so information that is unremarkable for one target may end a career or damage social standing for another. Looking only at whether information was previously private does not identify these contextual factors, which affect the harms and severity of a doxxing.<br><br>While [deanonymising and delegitimising doxxing can serve the public interest by exposing wrongdoing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-016-9406-0), and all three forms of doxxing can be unjustified where they humiliate, threaten, intimidate, or punish the subject.<br><br><b>Doxxing as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>Safeguard Defenders records that an anonymous account [doxxed the old home address of Li Ying, known online as Teacher Li, called on local Chinese students to go and find him, and later published images of his old and new passports](https://safeguarddefenders.com/en/blog/teacher-li-full-transnational-repression-story). He began moving frequently from that point. That case shows some of the harm produced by doxxing. While abuse can be blocked, muted, and ignored, a published address cannot be unpublished, and it does not stop working when the campaign ends; the target's situation changes permanently.<br><br><b>Potential Impacts:</b><br>Impacts are organised by affected party. An item may appear under more than one party where the justification differs.<br><br><b>Primary Target:</b><br><b>A1012.009: Subject Forced to Relocate.</b> Teacher Li began moving frequently once his former address circulated, and relocation is the only remedy available for an address that cannot be recalled.<br><b>A1011.003: Activity Restricts Subject's Freedom of Movement and Residence.</b> Targets change routes, avoid predictable places, and stop attending things once they can be found at them.<br><b>A1011.010: Activity Compromises Subject's Personal Data.</b> Publishing a name, address, or employer discloses personal data without consent, and a published detail cannot be unpublished.<br><b>A1012.006: Subject Killed.</b> Swatting is impossible without an address, and [fatal cases have been documented.](https://rist.tech.cornell.edu/papers/sok-abuse.pdf)<br><b>A1005.006: Activity Exhausts Subject's Resources.</b> Address removal services, legal advice, relocation costs, and physical security are recurring expenses rather than a single response.<br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> The target does not know who holds the information or what any of them will do, and the uncertainty does not end when the campaign does. [IT for Change's guide on online violence, gender, and law](https://projects.itforchange.net/online-violence-gender-and-law-guide/module-2-typologies-of-online-gender-based-offenses-in-law/2-8-doxxing/) lists offline stalking, death and rape threats, harassment of family and employers, physical harm, and the sending of offensive substances to the target's address among the consequences that follow from an address moving.<br><b>A1008.006: Feeling of Anxiety Evoked in Subject.</b> A published doxx remains available and can be acted on years later by people unconnected to the original actor.<br><b>A1010.001: Subject Disengages from Information Ecosystem.</b> [Researchers found that social network accounts named in dox files were more likely to close than typical accounts](https://www.cs.uic.edu/%7Epsnyder/static/papers/fifteen-minutes.pdf).<br><b>A1010.006: Subject Self-Censors when Sharing Information.</b> Targets remove biographical detail, stop posting photographs, and avoid anything that could be matched against what is already out.<br><b>A1011.002: Activity Restricts Subject's Freedom of Expression.</b> The restriction follows from the target's own risk calculation rather than from any prohibition.<br><br><b>People Connected to the Target:</b><br><b>A1008.017: Feeling of Fear Evoked in Subject.</b> Dox files routinely include family members and partners, so people who were never the object of the campaign become reachable through it.<br><b>A1012.009: Subject Forced to Relocate.</b> A household relocates together, so the remedy falls on everyone at the address rather than on the target alone.<br><br><b>Wider Audience:</b><br><b>A1007.002: Activity Dissuades Action.</b> A doxx demonstrates that pseudonymity is not protection, and people who relied on it draw the conclusion.<br><b>A1010.004: Subject Declines to Start Sharing Information.</b> Those who have not yet spoken decide not to, having seen that speaking can be followed by being locatable.<br><b>A1009.002: Degrade Quality of Information Ecosystem.</b> The aggregate of people who withdraw or write anonymously because identification carries physical risk.</td>

<td>Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</td>
<td><b>Summary:</b><br><i>Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</i> describes an actor identifying a person to an audience in circumstances where that audience is likely to act against them as a consequence of that identification. For example, posting a target to a forum whose members are known to conduct raids, an account with a large following inciting outrage at a target, publishing their name and employer, and a head of state characterising a journalist as a criminal. What identifies the Technique is that the actor supplies the target without needing an explicit call to harass; the audience of their identification goes on to supply the harassment, leaving the actor without direct culpability for what follows.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br><i>Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</i> is one form of harassment, and is something that often leads to further harassment from said audience. An incident has to satisfy four conditions before it is recorded as this Technique.<br><br><b>A target.</b> There is a target, and it is a person, a group of people, or an institution. The target is identified by the actor in some way to an audience. The target doesn't require being explicitly named as long as they are identifiable to the audience.<br><br><b>Identification to an audience.</b> The actor communicated the target's identity, or information sufficient to locate or reach them, to people other than the target. An account handle, a photograph, an employer, a school, a home address, and a schedule are each sufficient identifiers on their own, and in most cases the actor supplies several.<br><br><b>The audience is not one the actor explicitly coordinates for harassment campaigns.</b> The audience must be one the actor has access to rather than one the actor explicitly runs. An actor who circulates a target list inside a network they trained and can task has not deployed this Technique; they have coordinated the deployment of <b>A3004.002: Harass en Masse</b>. Part of the essence of <b>A3004.004: Identify a Target to an Audience Likely to Act</b> is that an actor isn't explicitly coordinating harassment. There is plausible deniability, but the identification can be assessed to have led to harassment of the target.<br><br>By identifying a target to an audience likely to act on that target (without having an operational relationship with the audience) an actor maintains plausible deniability, as an audience ostensibly acts on its own motivation. This identification can be a form of harassment, which instigates mass harassment to the identified target by the audience of the identification. The deniability of culpability is a unique outcome of this Technique, which is not possible for traditional coordination in which an actor explicitly organises a collaborating audience to participate in mass harassment of a target.<br><br><b>Awareness of the consequence of identification to the audience, subjective or objective.</b> Either the actor intentionally sought an audience to act against the target, or a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew would have understood the audience to be likely to act. The first criterion covers cases where an analyst is able to identify an actor's explicit motive (e.g. through actor statements). The second covers actors who published a name to a community who went on to harass, while the actor claims no responsibility or awareness, where a reasonable person could have predicted to harass the target based on the identification.<br><br>The second branch works from information the actor had available to them rather than from what they say they intended to happen. It asks what a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew would have understood. The first time an actor names a target and a mob arrives, the claim not to have expected it is available to them. By the tenth time it is not, because the pattern is part of the information they hold. For analysts, establishing that a publication caused a specific threat is challenging, but establishing that an actor has named targets repeatedly, and that harassment has repeatedly followed, is more achievable.<br><br>There are actors who, aware of the potential for their posts to lead to mass harassment, take steps to reduce the chance this happens. Callout posts can carry an instruction not to contact the person, a screenshot can have identifying details removed, conduct can be described without the person being named, and a target can be given notice before publication. Fanlore (a wiki site dedicated to fandoms) records that [many callout posts of the 2020s carry the phrase "do not harass this user"](https://fanlore.org/wiki/Callout_Culture) as a direction to block rather than engage. Whether such a disclaimer works is disputed, and an analyst should not treat it as though it does. What it does establish is that the actor contemplated the harassment, since nobody warns against a consequence they have not thought of.<br><br><b>How the Literature Refers to This Behaviour:</b><br>There is no settled term for this Technique in the literature. The nearest named concept is "stochastic terrorism", which describes the same target identification but resulting in physical violence rather than harassment. Some define stochastic terrorism as [the use of mass media to provoke acts that are statistically predictable but individually unpredictable](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17539153.2024.2305742), and others describe it as [raising the probability of violence without direct commands, through a diffusion of responsibility](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ftpvxx/v36y2024i2p234-245.html). This definition has been further developed to include repetition, describing it as the [repeated use of vilifying rhetoric by a public figure that inspires supporters to act](https://www.britannica.com/topic/stochastic-terrorism), and notes that the arrangement generally fails the legal test for incitement because the speech lacks sufficient specificity.<br><br>A criticism of the concept of stochastic terrorism is that it lets an accuser assert causation without demonstrating it, and that the accusation of stochastic terrorism can be used to suppress valid criticism. Analysts making the assessment that this Technique has been deployed will need to be able to justify the link between identification of a target and resulting harassment, and to balance individuals' right to express criticism of named targets with the documented use of target identification to direct harassment.<br><br>Another way this behaviour is referred to is "weaponised visibility" leading to "digital vigilantism", the latter of which is described as [citizens being collectively offended by another citizen's activity and responding through coordinated retaliation](https://philpapers.org/rec/TRODVA). In such a case visibility of the target is itself a weapon used against them.<br><br>Amy Boyer is an individual who has both participated in and been the target of this behaviour which they refer to as "callouts". They describe many callouts as necessary ("people need to be informed of those who are a danger to them"), but also acknowledge that many are abusive, and those that are abusive have common elements. As a consequence of this experience, they have produced [a list of signs that a callout may be abusive](https://medium.com/@amytranscend/theres-been-a-lot-of-important-talk-lately-about-how-callout-culture-can-actually-be-used-to-harm-543b13ce5add).<br><br><b>Examples of Identifying a Target to an Audience Likely to Act:</b><br>In October 2019 Coleen Rooney posted to a combined following of almost two million on Twitter and Instagram that she had planted false stories visible to a single account and watched them reach the press, concluding by naming Rebekah Vardy's account. Vardy sued for libel and lost in July 2022, with [Steyn J finding that the essence of the post was substantially true](https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/12661132/wagatha-christie-rebekah-vardy-loses-libel-case-against-coleen-rooney). Vardy said her family had received abuse and threats as a result, and the same judgment [recognised the vile abuse Vardy had faced and held that nothing she had been accused of could justify subjecting her and her family to such vitriol](https://theconversation.com/wagatha-christie-what-the-judgment-said-and-what-it-means-for-future-libel-litigants-187955). The judge also [agreed that it was not in the public interest for the disclosure to have been made without giving Vardy the chance to respond](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2022-07-30/wagatha-christie-trial-ends-with-rebekah-vardy-backfire/101284902).<br><br>To assess whether this behaviour meets the requirements for this Technique, an analyst must assess whether Rooney knew her behaviour would lead to harassment of Vardy, or that given the information available to her, it's reasonable to say that she should have been aware this would happen. While no court, regulator, or investigator has made a claim to this effect, Rooney published to roughly two million people across three platforms, naming one person as having betrayed her over years, in October 2019, by which point pile-ons following celebrity callouts were well documented. A reasonable person knowing what she knew would plausibly have understood that her audience would be likely to act on her identification. As such, the criteria for applying this Technique is assessed to have been met.<br><br>A second example looks at a case where a government official is repeatedly singled out by an individual leading an organisation the official is attempting to regulate. On 15 April 2024 Bishop Mar Mari Emmanuel was stabbed repeatedly during a livestreamed sermon at the Assyrian Christ the Good Shepherd Church in Wakeley, in western Sydney, and [a riot followed outside the church that night in which police were injured and vehicles damaged](https://www.startupdaily.net/topic/politics-news-analysis/elon-musk-1-esafety-commissioner-0-in-legal-battle-over-priest-stabbing-video-removal-on-x/). A sixteen-year-old was later charged with a federal terrorism offence. Footage circulated immediately on X, and Australia's eSafety Commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, issued removal notices after the material was [classified Class 1 under the Online Safety Act](https://au.news.yahoo.com/musk-court-win-over-stabbing-004100383.html). [Meta, TikTok, Reddit, and Telegram took the video down when asked](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/australia-drops-court-action-x-church-stabbing-posts-rcna155553). X geo-blocked the posts for Australian users and refused to remove them globally, and [eSafety went to court arguing that geo-blocking was not compliant with the law because the content had not been deleted](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-04-22/esafety-commissioner-seeks-injunction-against-x/103755874).<br><br>Throughout the dispute Elon Musk characterised Inman Grant as a global censorship commissar. On the day she discontinued the case against X, she said Musk had ["issued a dog whistle to 181 million users around the globe"](https://region.com.au/esafety-drops-federal-court-action-against-x-with-commissioner-claiming-deaths-threats-made/776431/), producing death threats against her and the doxxing of her family including her three children. She has described threats to her employees, her family, and her own safety, credible enough that she involved federal and local police and changed her movements, and a Columbia University study of technology-facilitated gender-based violence used her as a case study and found her [mentioned in almost 74,000 posts on X ahead of the court proceedings, having been a relatively unknown figure online beforehand](https://ghanaiantimes.com.gh/these-arent-just-words-the-woman-threatened-for-taking-x-to-court/). Both the Communications Minister and the Opposition Leader condemned the abuse in Parliament.<br><br>Musk used the same characterisation of Inman Grant again in a second regulatory dispute eighteen months later. Following the announcement of Australia's under-16 social media ban, which Inman Grant was to implement, he again posted calling her a censorship commissar, and [within 24 hours 75,000 posts were directed at her, 80% of them toxic, harmful, and plausible death threats](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/esafety-commissioner-julie-inman-grant-death-thrats-social-media/t1rkjtpqv). She has since said her own account is dormant and [that eSafety no longer has a handle at all](https://www.forbes.com.au/covers/leadership/inman-grant-the-personal-toll-of-being-elon-musks-censorship-commissar/).<br><br>The assessment of the actor's awareness of the outcome of singling out a target runs differently here. Rooney's case rests on general knowledge about what follows a celebrity callout. Musk's rests on his own record against the same target. The first time he singled out Inman Grant led to death threats, the doxxing of three children, police involvement, and condemnation in Parliament, all of which were public by June 2024. Whatever claim of surprise was available before that was not available in November, because by then the result of characterising this official in this way had been established, reported, and debated in the legislature. Further, as the owner of a social media platform, Musk is more aware than most of the trust and safety issues associated with target identification and their resulting harassment.<br><br>A reasonable person with access to the same information would have understood the audience to be likely to act, and the claim not to have anticipated it is not available to an actor who has watched it happen already. No public evidence establishes that he sought the outcome, but given the information known to be available to him, it does not need to be. As such, the Technique is assessed to have been deployed here. However, that assessment takes no position on the validity of the criticism. Contesting a regulator's exercise of censorship powers is within the bounds of legitimate free speech, and Musk was in litigation with her agency at the time. That and the documented consequence for Inman Grant are both true at once.</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A3004.005.md">A3004.005</a></td>
<td>Disrupt Event</td>
<td><b>Summary:</b><br><i>Disrupt Event</i> describes action taken against a specific convened occasion which interferes with that occasion's ability to do what it was convened to do. The action may stop the event happening, prevent it proceeding as planned, or turn it to a purpose other than the one it was called for. In this Technique an event, rather than a person or a body of content, is the thing being acted on.<br><br><b>Detail:</b><br>For the purposes of this Technique, an event is a bounded occasion which has a convenor, a purpose, an intended audience, and a scheduled time. Conferences, panel discussions, book launches, livestreams, religious services, court hearings, public consultation meetings, graduation ceremonies, ticket sales, and community group meetings all meet this description. An event may be physical, online, or both. Identifying the affected event is a precondition for identifying this Technique. Behaviour which makes life difficult for an organisation in general is not this Technique; behaviour aimed at a particular occasion that organisation had scheduled is.<br><br>An event is disrupted when a participant acts on it in a way that interferes with its ability to fulfil its purpose. The test is functional rather than intentional. An analyst does not need to establish why the actor did it before recording that the Technique was deployed, and tagging this Technique makes no claim as to whether the disruption was just or unjust.<br><br>The Technique covers action at any point in an event's life:<br><br>The event is stopped before it happens. Pressure is applied to the convenor, the venue, the funder, or the host institution, and the occasion is cancelled or the participants who gave it its purpose are removed from it.<br>The event happens but cannot proceed as convened. The proceedings are interrupted, the participants are prevented from hearing or from being heard, or the infrastructure the occasion depends on is attacked.<br>The event happens and serves a purpose other than its own. Control of the occasion passes to the disrupting party, and what the audience receives is what that party brought rather than what the event originally intended to deliver.<br><br>Two situations resemble a disrupted event and are not this Technique. The first is an event that fails with nobody acting on it. A power cut, a speaker's illness, a platform outage, or a venue's own capacity limit will each wreck an occasion without any participant having taken action against it. The second is participation. Attending, listening, asking a hostile question, applauding, refusing to applaud, and walking out are all things audiences do at events, and doing them does not disrupt the occasion. The line falls where a participant's use of the event stops the event working. Asking a long question is participation; holding the floor until the session ends so that nobody else can speak is disruption.<br><br><b>Disrupting an Event as a Method of Information Suppression:</b><br>In July 2014 the European Association for Chinese Studies held its biennial conference at the University of Minho in Braga, Portugal. On the evening of 22 July, Xu Lin, then Director-General of Confucius Institute Headquarters, reviewed the conference materials and ordered them seized. [Four pages were removed](https://chinesestudies.eu/2014/report-the-deletion-of-pages-from-eacs-conference-materials-in-braga-july-2014/) from the printed programme and abstracts, covering the Chiang Ching-kuo Foundation's presentation of itself as a sponsor, an exhibition by Taiwan's National Central Library, and the associated speaker biographies and event listings. Incomplete materials went out to delegates from 23 July and defaced programmes followed on the morning of 24 July. Roger Greatrex, the EACS president, had 500 replacement pages printed and distributed. This is disruption of an event in progress, carried out on its physical materials. No session was interrupted and nothing was said to the delegates from the platform; what changed was that an attempt was made to make it so that the conference could no longer tell its attendees who had paid for it or what else was on.<br><br>Disruption can also take only part of an event, such as speaker questions. On 24 January 2019 the University of Strasbourg hosted a panel on China's policies in Xinjiang, organised by Thomas Boutonnet, with two Uyghur scholars among the four speakers. The consulate general had already approached the university president to have the event banned, and the president had refused. When the panel opened its audience discussion, [two participants took the available time](https://lingua-sinica.org/the-limits-of-talking-about-china-in-europe/) and left the speakers no opportunity to respond; Boutonnet and colleagues later identified them as visitors from China's consulate general in Strasbourg. Boutonnet let this run for several minutes before intervening. "We need to let others speak," he said. "We have to let the discussion flow. Otherwise, it's simply not possible." No rule of the venue was broken and the event was not stopped, but both the attempts to ban the event and to monopolise the audience questions were done with the goal of disrupting the event by actors associated with Chinese authorities.<br><br>Disruption can target specific parts of an event rather than all of it. In 2024 the Baltic Alliance for Asian Studies conference at the University of Latvia told confirmed participants, less than two weeks before it opened, that they were no longer welcome to present. Raigirdas Boruta, whose paper concerned Taiwan, said the [common element among those rejected](https://lingua-sinica.org/the-limits-of-talking-about-china-in-europe/) was that their work touched subjects sensitive to Beijing. It later emerged that the Chinese Embassy in Riga had funded the conference. The Centre for Asian Studies at Vytautas Magnus University withdrew, and its six scheduled speakers withdrew with it. The conference took place. What did not take place was the portion of it that had been assembled around Taiwan.<br><br>Pressure applied to institutions can lead to events altering their initial intended purpose. ARTICLE 19 has [catalogued European cultural institutions](https://www.article19.org/resources/europe-chinas-censorship-of-cultural-institutions-must-be-challenged/) dropping or changing programming under Chinese pressure, including exhibitions of work by the artist Badiucao in Poland, Italy, and the Czech Republic. The Château des Ducs de Bretagne in Nantes postponed a Genghis Khan exhibition after Chinese partners demanded the removal of words including "Mongolia" and "Empire", and reopened it later without the Chinese partnership.<br><br>Disruption can also be attempted against the technical infrastructure an event runs on. Italian police reported [mitigating denial of service attacks](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/entertainment-arts-61463364) by the pro-Russian group Killnet against network infrastructure during the first semi-final of the 2022 Eurovision Song Contest, in which Ukraine performed, and during the final's voting. The attacks failed. This is the Technique deployed with no impact to record against it; an actor acted on a specific scheduled occasion in a way capable of stopping it working, and the occasion worked anyway.<br><br><b>Disrupting as a Method of Harassment:</b><br>Chen Ling, Utkucan Balcı, Jeremy Blackburn, and Gianluca Stringhini identified [over 200 calls for zoombombing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03822) on Twitter and 4chan across the first seven months of 2020, covering ten online meeting platforms. Their central finding was that most attacks did not come from outsiders guessing meeting IDs. They came from people with legitimate access who published the details and invited attackers in. Password protection and waiting rooms are ineffective against an insider, and the authors found attackers being coached on how to present themselves in order to be admitted.<br><br>On 21 April 2020 the Israeli embassy in Berlin held an online Holocaust Remembrance Day ceremony with the survivor Tswi Joseef Herschel. About twenty minutes in, [anonymous participants posted photographs of Hitler and pornographic images](https://www.timesofisrael.com/anti-semites-break-into-virtual-holocaust-memorial-hosted-by-israeli-embassy/) and shouted antisemitic slogans. The session was suspended and reconvened without them, and a second ceremony was held the following day on Facebook Live behind a password.<br><br>Online twelve-step recovery meetings were attacked repeatedly through the same period. Intruders [displayed pornography, racial slurs, and antisemitic material](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/zoom-bombing-alcoholics-anonymous-meetings-coronavirus/), and in one case an attacker took control through screen sharing and returned each time he was removed. Access details reached them because groups had published meeting IDs on websites and social media so that anyone who needed a meeting could find one.<br><br>A threat can disrupt an event as effectively as an intrusion, and costs the actor less. GLAAD, working with the Anti-Defamation League's Center on Extremism, counted [161 protests and threats against drag events](https://glaad.org/anti-drag-report) in the United States between the start of 2022 and the end of March 2023, drawn from media reports across all fifty states and acknowledged as an undercount. Bomb threats closed an Indianapolis bookstore and emptied a Louisville venue in March 2023. A hoax threat disrupts an event whether or not a device exists, because the response to the threat is what clears the room. Swatting a livestream works the same way; a false emergency report brings [armed police to the broadcaster's address](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/technology-28970473) while the broadcast is running.<br><br><b>Establishing that an Event was Disrupted:</b><br>In June 2020 users on TikTok, joined by K-pop fan accounts, encouraged people to reserve tickets for Donald Trump's rally at the BOK Center in Tulsa with no intention of attending, so that seats would stand empty. Mary Jo Laupp's video making the suggestion [passed two million views](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2020-06-22/did-tiktok-users-kpop-fans-really-troll-donald-trump-tulsa-rally/12378768). The campaign claimed a million ticket requests for a venue seating around 19,000, and roughly 6,200 people attended. Brad Parscale said that registration amounted to an RSVP with a phone number, that the campaign filtered out false numbers, and that inflated requests never entered its planning. The pandemic is an obvious competing explanation for the turnout, and the reporting did not settle the question.<br><br>The attempt at deploying the Technique should be documented here, but the lack of impact should also be recorded. An analyst can record that a coordinated campaign acted on a scheduled occasion without recording that the occasion's attendance was reduced, and should do exactly that whenever the second claim is unsupported. Organisers have an incentive to attribute a poor event to sabotage, and disrupting parties have an incentive to claim credit for outcomes they did not cause. Neither claim is evidence of an impact.<br><br><b>Events which Fail without being Disrupted:</b><br>On 15 November 2022 Ticketmaster's presale for Taylor Swift's Eras Tour collapsed under demand, and two days later the [general public sale was cancelled](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/11/17/fridays-public-ticket-sale-for-taylor-swift-eras-tour-canceled-after-extreme-demand.html) for want of remaining inventory. Live Nation's chairman, Greg Maffei, said that around 1.5 million verified fans had been invited, that some 14 million people hit the site, and that demand would have filled 900 stadiums. Bot traffic was named as part of the problem. The sale did not function, and the audience it was built for could not get what it came for.<br><br>This is not an example of the Technique being deployed. The people who broke the sale were the people the sale existed for, doing the thing the sale existed for them to do. No participant acted against the occasion; its own capacity met genuine demand and lost. An operator's statement that bots were responsible is a claim about the presence of an actor, and an analyst has to test that claim rather than adopt it.</td>
<td>TA85</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A8004.001.md">A8004.001</a></td>
<td>Intended Target of Activity</td>
<td>Summary: The subject is assessed to be one the actor intended to impact.<br><br>Detail: A8004.001: Intended Target of Activity records that the actor set out to produce an impact on this participant.<br><br>Several participants can be intended targets of one activity. <td>TA92</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><a href="techniques/A8004.002.md">A8004.002</a></td>
<td>Proxy Target of Activity</td>
<td>Summary: The subject is assessed to have been targeted in order to impact an intended target.<br><br>Detail: A8004.002: Proxy Target of Activity records a participant the actor acted upon in order to bring about an impact on another subject.<br><br>One example of the usage of a proxy target is in the Technique A3001.001: Coercion by Proxy; a proxy is harmed or threatened to apply pressure to an intended target. The actors targeted the outlets with impersonation, and this activity was used to achieve impacts on further targets who believed these impersonations were genuine.</td>
<td>TA92</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A8006.001.md">A8006.001</a></td>
<td>Direct Recipient of Activity</td>
<td>Summary: The activity was delivered to, or carried out on, the participant individually.<br><br>Detail: A8006.001: Direct Recipient of Activity records that activity was directly carried out on a subject. [The Office of the Director of National Intelligence and the FBI attributed the operation to Iran on 21 October 2020.](https://www.politifact.com/article/2020/oct/22/federal-officials-say-iran-sent-email-threats-vote/)<br><br>This case demonstrates direct delivery in a state-linked operation. Each recipient was addressed individually from their own registration record, so every voter who received an email is a direct recipient. <td>TA92</td>

<td><a href="techniques/A8006.002.md">A8006.002</a></td>

<td>Summary: The participant perceived the activity without it having been delivered to or directed at them individually.<br><br>Detail: A8006.002: Observer of Activity records that the participant became aware of the activity by some route other than being directly acted upon in the incident.<br><br>It covers seeing a publication in a place the participant had access to, and learning of the activity secondhand from reporting or from another person. <td>TA92</td>
</tr>

<td><a href="techniques/A9001.md">A9001</a></td>
<td>Impact Duration</td>
<td>Summary: This category contains assessments used to document the assessed duration of an impact.<br><br>Detail: This category contains assessments used to document how long an impact lasted, measured from the point at which an impact first manifested.<br><br>Duration is independent of how the activity was conducted. A single action can produce a permanent change and a campaign running for years can produce nothing which outlives it, so the persistence of the effect has to be assessed separately from the persistence of the effort.<br><br>For impacts assessed as harm, duration is the closest available proxy for severity. For impacts assessed as benefit, duration answers the campaign effectiveness question, because an actor who moves opinion for a fortnight has achieved something different from one who moves it for a decade.<br><br>A9001.001: Fleeting Impact and A9001.002: Lasting Impact are differentiated by whether the impact outlasted the activity which caused it. A9001.002: Lasting Impact and A9001.003: Permanent Impact are differentiated by whether the impact can be undone. An impact which has potential to return to its prior state, or has already done so after an extended period of time is lasting; one which cannot return to its prior state is permanent.</td>
<td>TA82</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><a href="techniques/A9001.001.md">A9001.001</a></td>
<td>Fleeting Impact</td>
<td>Summary: Impact has been assessed to have resolved immediately or shortly after the associated activity was completed.<br><br>Detail: This value records an impact which did not outlast the activity which caused it. The subject, market, or audience returned to something close to its prior state immediately or shortly after the associated activity was completed.<br><br>A fleeting impact is not a failed operation. An actor whose objective was a brief market movement, a short-lived distraction, or a delay of a few hours has succeeded. This assessment records the duration of the effect rather than its adequacy.<br><br>Example<br><br>On 23 April 2013 the Associated Press Twitter account, which had close to two million followers, [posted that there had been two explosions at the White House and that President Obama had been injured](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/worldviews/wp/2013/04/23/syrian-hackers-claim-ap-hack-that-tipped-stock-market-by-136-billion-is-it-terrorism/). The account had been compromised following attempts to steal AP journalists' passwords, and [the Syrian Electronic Army claimed responsibility](https://foreignpolicy.com/2013/04/23/syrian-electronic-army-takes-credit-for-hacking-ap-twitter-account/). The Dow fell 143 points within minutes, from 14,697 to 14,554, with [reports claiming the swing erased around 136 billion dollars in market value in 3 minutes.](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/worldviews/wp/2013/04/23/syrian-hackers-claim-ap-hack-that-tipped-stock-market-by-136-billion-is-it-terrorism/) The White House press secretary confirmed the president was unharmed, and the market recovered as soon as the claim was understood to be false. This case demonstrates a substantial but temporary impact.</td>
<td>TA82</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><a href="techniques/A9001.002.md">A9001.002</a></td>
<td>Lasting Impact</td>
<td>Summary: Impact has been assessed to have outlasted the activity which caused it, but has not been established as an irreversible, permanent change.<br><br>Detail: This value records an impact which outlived the activity but has not been established as permanent.<br><br>A campaign ending in March and an effect still measurable in September is lasting, however long the campaign itself ran. The effect does not have to be continuing at the point of assessment, only to have extended past the activity.<br><br>Where the impact cannot be undone, use A9001.003: Permanent Impact instead. The difference is not how long the impact has run but whether the prior state can be returned to.<br><br>Example<br><br>From mid-2003 religious and political figures in northern Nigeria promoted claims that oral polio vaccine was contaminated with anti-fertility agents and HIV, and [five northern states suspended its use between July 2003 and August 2004](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4098042/). Zamfara, Kaduna, Bauchi, and Niger rejoined national immunisation days within a few months, while [Kano state did not allow vaccination to resume until a year later](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.0040073). [Reported polio cases in Nigeria rose from 202 in 2002 to 1,143 in 2006, and Nigerian strains of the virus spread across Africa and beyond](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4098042/). Nigeria's last wild poliovirus case had onset in Borno State in August 2016, and [the WHO African region was certified free of wild poliovirus in August 2020](https://www.afro.who.int/news/africa-eradicates-wild-poliovirus).<br><br>This case demonstrates an impact which ran for more than a decade after an activity ended, and which was then reversed. Transmission continued long after vaccination resumed, so the effect is not co-extensive with the boycott. Certification in 2020 evidences that the prior state was recoverable. The individual paralysis cases inside this outbreak are not recoverable, and would be recorded separately against a physical harm impact carrying A9001.003: Permanent Impact.</td>
<td>TA82</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><a href="techniques/A9001.003.md">A9001.003</a></td>
<td>Permanent Impact</td>
<td>Summary: Impact has been assessed to have continued indefinitely, or to have altered the subject's circumstances permanently.<br><br>Detail: This value records an impact which cannot be undone. The change is settled, and the subject, situation, or audience will not return to the state it held before the activity.<br><br>Some outcomes are irreversible by their nature. A death, an injury which does not heal, or the publication of private material cannot be taken back, so the value follows from what the outcome is rather than from a forecast about what happens next. Others become irreversible through time. Years spent out of a profession and the income which went with them are not recoverable, whatever formal position is reached later. Assess against what has been lost rather than against what remains theoretically possible.<br><br>Where the impact has not been confirmed to be irreversible, use A9001.002: Lasting Impact instead, however long the impact has run so far. An impact which has held for years and can still be undone is lasting rather than permanent.<br><br>Example<br><br>From August 2017 Myanmar security forces conducted a campaign against the Rohingya in Rakhine State. They unlawfully killed thousands of Rohingya, including young children; raped and committed other sexual violence against Rohingya women and girls; tortured Rohingya men and boys in detention sites; and burned down hundreds of Rohingya villages. During this period more than 700,000 people fled to Bangladesh. Amnesty International's September 2022 report The Social Atrocity found that [Meta's engagement-based algorithms proactively amplified content inciting violence and discrimination against the Rohingya in the years before the violence](https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/asa16/5933/2022/en/), and recorded that in mid-2014 the company had one Burmese-speaking content moderator, based in Dublin, for 1.2 million users in Myanmar. As the ninth anniversary of the exodus approached, [around 1.2 million Rohingya refugees were living in Bangladesh in June 2026, most of them in camps in Cox's Bazar.](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/06/1167623)<br><br>This case demonstrates an impact which is irreversible on several counts. The deaths cannot be undone, and nearly nine years of displacement cannot be returned even if repatriation eventually proceeds. The permanency does not rest on predicting that return will never happen; rather on the time already lost.</td>
<td>TA82</td>
</tr>
